# Chapter 12: The Person You're Becoming

---

The last day before winter break was a half day.

Teachers gave up on teaching by third period. The hallways buzzed with the particular energy of three hundred kids who could smell freedom — two weeks of no alarms, no lockers, no hallway rivers, no cafeteria calculations. The building felt different. Lighter. Like the walls knew they were about to rest.

Aran walked through Lincoln Middle School and felt the building around him. Three stories of beige brick. Too many doors. All of them, now, familiar.

Room 217, where Mrs. Park taught English and where Aran had sat on his first day, hands rehearsing. Room 104, where Mr. Okonkwo's math problems had sent him reaching for his phone. Room 310, where Mr. Tanaka stood at his board with his whiteboard marker and his exactness and his quiet refusal to accept anything less than what you could do.

He knew these rooms. He knew the hallways between them — which ones were faster, which ones had the broken water fountain, which ones had the eighth-graders who no longer looked at him like a fly but like a feature of the landscape, unremarkable and accepted.

The building hadn't gotten smaller. But he had gotten bigger. Mani had been right about that, the way Mani was right about most things: simply, practically, without fanfare.

---

Last period was Mr. Tanaka's class. The final class before break. Mr. Tanaka, who didn't do parties or games or movie days, did something unexpected: he gave them a writing prompt.

"Write about a system you've observed this semester. Not a scientific system — a personal one. A system in your own life that you've watched, studied, or changed."

He handed out blank paper. No lines. No prompts. Just the white space and the instruction.

Aran looked at the paper. Three months ago, blank paper had felt like a demand — fill me, perform, produce. Now it felt like a workbench. Clean. Ready. Waiting for the builder.

He picked up his pencil.

---

*A Personal System*
*by Aran Rajan*

*The system I want to write about is myself.*

*This might sound strange, but Mr. Tanaka said to write about a system I've observed, and the system I've observed most closely this semester is the one I live inside. My brain, my body, my choices, and the way they interact.*

*At the start of the year, I thought character was something you either had or didn't. Like height, or eye color, or being good at sports. Some people were brave. Some people were honest. Some people were patient. You were born with it or you weren't, and the best you could do was pretend to have the traits you were missing.*

*I was wrong. Character isn't a trait. It's a system.*

He stopped. Looked at what he'd written. It was honest, but it wasn't finished. It wasn't the kind of thing Mr. Tanaka would accept as a complete answer — it was the kind of thing Mr. Tanaka would push. "What do you mean by 'system'? Be specific. Show your work."

Aran continued:

*A system has inputs, processes, and outputs. The input is the situation — the moment that asks you to choose. The process is the choosing — the wobble, the voice, the impulse, the quiet. The output is the action — what you actually do.*

*What I've learned this semester is that you can't control the input. Situations arrive. People are mean. Tests are hard. Wallets appear on sidewalks. Friends cheat. Holidays hurt. The world delivers its inputs on its own schedule, and you don't get a menu.*

*But you can work on the process. You can practice the choosing. Not by being perfect — I'm not perfect, and the semester has a long highlight reel of wobbles and shortcuts and walks-when-I-should-have-run. But by showing up to the process, again and again, the way the water cycle shows up to the rock. Drop by drop. Choice by choice.*

*And the output changes. Not because the person changed overnight. Because the system improved. Better inputs (I put myself in harder situations on purpose now — morning runs, hard conversations, teaching someone younger). Better processing (the wobble is louder; the voice is quieter; the gap between impulse and action is wider). Better outputs (I kept forty-seven dollars that wasn't mine for zero seconds. Three months ago, I don't know how long it would have taken).*

*This is the system. This is the personal one I've been studying. Not in a lab. In the hallway, and the cafeteria, and the shop, and my own room at night with a notebook and a lamp.*

He put his pencil down. Mr. Tanaka was walking the rows, reading over shoulders with the practiced non-interference of a teacher who trusted the process. He paused at Aran's desk, read for a moment, and moved on. No comment. No correction. Just the pause — the Mr. Tanaka version of "solid methodology."

---

After school, Aran walked to Mani's shop one last time before break.

The shop was quiet. End of year. Most customers had picked up their cars. The bays were empty. Mani was at his workbench, doing what he did when there were no cars: cleaning tools.

Each tool was wiped, oiled, and returned to its place on the pegboard. The pegboard had outlines — shadows of each tool drawn in marker, so that every wrench, every socket, every screwdriver had a specific home. When all the tools were in place, the pegboard looked like a diagram. When one was missing, the shadow showed you exactly what was gone.

"Last day," Aran said.

"Last day of what?"

"School. Before break."

"Ah." Mani wiped a box-end wrench — 14mm, his most used — and hung it in its shadow. "How was it?"

"Good. Mr. Tanaka asked us to write about a personal system."

"What did you write about?"

"Me."

"You're a system?"

"Everything's a system. You taught me that."

Mani made the sound — the half-laugh, half-cough that meant he was amused and trying not to show it. "I taught you how to hand me a 10-mil socket. The rest you figured out."

"You taught me about alignment. And brake pads. And patience. And the oil filter. And torque versus force. And the star pattern. And the grooves."

"I taught you about cars."

"You taught me about cars. And every time, the cars turned out to be about something else."

Mani hung the last wrench. The pegboard was full — every tool in its shadow, every shadow filled. Complete. Aligned.

He looked at Aran. A long look. The kind of look a mechanic gives an engine after a full tune-up — checking the idle, listening to the rhythm, making sure everything is where it should be.

"You're different than you were in September," Mani said.

"I know."

"Not bigger. Well, maybe bigger. But that's not what I mean. You're... steadier. Like an engine that's been tuned. The idle is smoother. The timing is right."

"The alignment is better."

"The alignment is better. Yeah."

They stood in the clean shop with the full pegboard and the empty bays and the December light coming through the garage door, low and golden, the kind of light that made everything look like it was being remembered.

"Uncle Mani?"

"Yeah."

"Thanks. For — all of it. The shop. The talks. The socket lessons."

"Kid, you handed me tools and swept the floor. I should be thanking you."

"You know it was more than that."

Mani was quiet for a moment. The quiet of a man who was not naturally verbal encountering an emotion that required words and not having the right wrench for it.

"Your dad asked me to keep an eye on you," he said. "When you moved here. He was worried — the new school, the separation, all of it. He said, 'Just let him hang around the shop. Let him get his hands dirty. He thinks better when his hands are busy.'"

"I didn't know that."

"You weren't supposed to. But your dad knows you. He knows you need to build things to understand things. The shop was his idea. I just left the door open."

Aran felt something in his chest — not the wobble, not the clench, not the heat. Something warmer. The warmth of learning that a system you thought was accidental was actually designed. That the door you'd walked through every afternoon had been opened on purpose, by a father who understood that his son needed a place to put his hands while his mind did the harder work.

"Tell Appa I said thanks," Aran said.

"Tell him yourself."

---

That evening, Aran sat at the kitchen table with Appa.

Dinner was done. Dishes were done. The house was quiet in the way it got on Friday evenings — the kind of quiet that wasn't empty, just still. Like a workshop between projects. Everything in its place. The next thing not started yet.

"Appa?"

"Hmm?"

"Thanks for Mani's shop."

Appa looked up from his laptop. The look was careful — not surprised, but careful. The look of a man whose quiet investment had been noticed.

"He told you?"

"He told me it was your idea."

Appa closed the laptop. Full attention. The Thatha-putting-down-his-tea equivalent.

"You were struggling," Appa said. "When we moved. I could see it. Not in what you said — you don't say much. In your hands. They were always moving. Opening and closing. Like they were looking for something to hold."

"I know. They do that when I'm overwhelmed."

"Mani's shop gave them something to hold. That was the idea. Not life lessons. Not character building. Just... a place where your hands had a job."

"But the life lessons happened anyway."

"They usually do. When the hands are busy, the mind relaxes. When the mind relaxes, it starts to see things."

Aran thought about this. All semester, he'd gone to Mani's shop thinking he was helping with cars. And he was. But the cars were the surface. Underneath the cars, his hands were learning to be still, his mind was learning to notice, and the lessons were leaking in sideways — through torque wrenches and oil filters and brake pads — because they couldn't come in the front door.

Some things can't be taught directly. They have to come through the side entrance, disguised as something else, while your hands are busy and your defenses are down.

"Appa, I need to tell you something."

"Okay."

"I know you worry. About me. About the separation. About whether I'm okay."

"I do."

"I'm okay. Not perfect. Not finished. But okay. Like — you know when a car comes in and it's running but it needs work? Not broken. Just needs tuning?"

Appa smiled. The smile of a man hearing his own child speak in a language learned from his brother, the mechanic, and finding it both unexpected and exactly right.

"I know that feeling," Appa said.

"That's me. Running. Needs tuning. But running."

"That's all of us, Aran."

"I know. That's the other thing I figured out this semester. It's not just me. It's everyone. Mani. Mr. Tanaka. Jay. You. Everyone is running and needs tuning. The only difference is whether you know it."

"And you know it."

"I'm starting to."

---

Later that night, Aran sat at his desk for the last time before break. The model plane was above him. The running shoes were by the door. The notebook was open to a fresh page.

He looked around his room and saw — not the objects, but the evidence. Evidence of a semester spent building.

The plane: patience. Do not force. Forty-seven pieces, nine evenings, each joint a decision to go slow.

The shoes: discipline. Seventy-five mornings. Same route. Same alarm. Same voice. Same choice.

The notebook: honesty. Eighty-something entries. Some good. Some confused. All true.

The shelf where the white ribbon sat — third place, science fair — small and unimpressive and real: the ability to lose without breaking.

He picked up his pen.

*Last entry before break.*

*I don't know who I'm becoming. I know who I was — the kid in September, counting doors, hands rehearsing, stomach clenching. I know who I am now — the kid in December, who runs mornings and fixes chains and tells his friend the hard truth and gives back wallets and sits with kids who are alone.*

*But who I'm BECOMING — that's still open. That's still being written. And the writing isn't done in moments of inspiration or grand gestures or speeches in front of crowds. The writing is done in the small things. The repeating things. The things nobody sees.*

*The morning run that nobody watches.*
*The wobble that nobody hears.*
*The wallet that nobody knows about.*
*The conversation that nobody graded.*
*The chain that clicks for a kid who fixed it herself.*

*Character isn't a monument. It's a house. You build it one board at a time, one nail at a time, one day at a time. And some of the boards are crooked and some of the nails bend and sometimes the whole thing looks like it's going to fall down.*

*But you keep building. Because the building is the point. Not the finished house. Not the blueprint. Not the approval of the building inspector. The building. The daily, boring, invisible act of putting one piece on top of another and making something that wasn't there before.*

*Uncle Mani taught me about cars. But really he taught me about this: everything that works was built by someone who showed up and did the small thing, again and again, until the small things became the whole thing.*

*Appa taught me about being steady. Not through speeches. Through dinner every night. Through the spreadsheet that was really love. Through the open door at Mani's shop that I didn't know he'd opened.*

*Mr. Tanaka taught me about exactness. Not as cruelty. As respect. The respect of believing someone is capable of doing something right and holding them to it.*

*Jay taught me about friendship that survives honesty. That the engine keeps running if you adjust it carefully.*

*Leela taught me that I know more than I think, and less than I assume, and the gap between those two is where teaching happens.*

*Owen taught me that sometimes the bravest thing is the quietest thing.*

*And the wobble — the wobble taught me that my body keeps score. That alignment is a feeling, not a formula. That I know when I'm true and I know when I'm off, and the knowing is the beginning of everything.*

*I'm eleven. I've got a lot of building left.*

*But the foundation is poured. The first walls are up. The roof isn't on yet, but you can stand inside and see the shape of the thing. It's not a mansion. It's not a masterpiece. It's a structure, built by a kid with greasy hands and a notebook and a pair of running shoes that smell terrible.*

*It's mine. I'm building it.*

*And tomorrow — even though tomorrow is break, even though tomorrow has no school and no obligations and no one checking — tomorrow at 6:15, the alarm will go off, and I'll put on my shoes, and I'll run.*

*Not because someone told me to.*

*Because that's who I'm building.*

---

He closed the notebook. Turned off the lamp. Got into bed.

The house was quiet. Appa was asleep — his breathing audible through the wall, steady and deep, the breathing of a man who had done his best today and would do his best tomorrow and understood that "best" was not perfect but was enough.

The model plane caught the streetlight — a faint amber glow on balsa wood, the ghost of nine evenings visible in its seams.

The running shoes sat by the door, waiting.

Outside, December held the neighborhood in its cold hands. The streetlights were on, one by one, the same pattern Aran had noticed on his Thanksgiving walk. The stars were there — behind the light, behind the cold, behind everything that was louder and brighter and more immediate.

Already there. Always there.

And somewhere inside an eleven-year-old boy with greasy hands and steady eyes and a chest that knew the difference between wobble and alignment, something was being built. Not finished. Not perfect. Not even halfway done.

But solid. And real. And his.

The character he was building.

One day at a time.

## Still Thinking?

- Aran says "character isn't a monument — it's a house. You build it one board at a time." If your character were a house, what's already built? What's still under construction?

- Appa opened the door to Mani's shop without telling Aran why. Some of the most important help comes sideways, disguised as something else. Has someone ever helped you in a way you didn't recognize until later?

- Aran lists what each person taught him — Mani about building, Appa about steadiness, Mr. Tanaka about exactness, Jay about honest friendship, Leela about teaching. Who are your teachers? What has each one built in you?

- "Tomorrow at 6:15, the alarm will go off, and I'll put on my shoes, and I'll run. Not because someone told me to. Because that's who I'm building." Is there a thing YOU do — or could start doing — not because someone told you to, but because of who you want to become?

- The book began with Aran counting doors. It ends with him building. What doors are you counting right now? What are you building?

*Aran's story continues wherever a kid puts on their shoes in the morning and chooses to run. Not because the road is easy. Because the running builds the runner.*
