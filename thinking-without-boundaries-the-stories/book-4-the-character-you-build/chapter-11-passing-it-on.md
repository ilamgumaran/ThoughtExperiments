# Chapter 11: Passing It On

---

Leela's bike chain broke on a Saturday in December.

Aran heard the sound from the front porch — the particular metallic snap of a chain parting company with its gears, followed by the scrape of a pedal spinning free, followed by a sound that was either a crash or a very aggressive dismount.

He walked to the sidewalk. Leela was standing over her bike with the expression of a general surveying a lost battle. She was eight, small for her age, and the bike was too big for her — a hand-me-down from an older sibling, the kind of bike that was technically rideable if you leaned forward and pedaled on your toes.

"You okay?" Aran asked.

"My bike is dead."

"Your bike isn't dead. Your chain came off."

"Same thing."

"Not even close."

He crouched down and looked at the chain. It hadn't broken — it had derailed, jumping off the front sprocket and wedging itself between the gear and the frame. A five-minute fix. Maybe ten, if the chain was tight.

"I can fix this," he said.

"Really?"

"It's a chain. It goes back on."

He'd learned this at Mani's shop — not bike chains specifically, but the principle was the same. Chain, sprocket, tension, alignment. Machines were machines. The scale changed but the physics didn't.

He put the chain back on. His fingers got greasy. Leela watched with the intensity of someone witnessing surgery.

"How did you know how to do that?"

"My uncle's a mechanic. He taught me about chains."

"Car chains?"

"Timing chains. Different but same idea."

Leela picked up the bike and tested the pedals. The chain caught, the wheel turned, and the bike was alive again. She looked at Aran like he'd performed a miracle, which he hadn't — he'd performed a basic mechanical repair — but the look was genuine, and genuine admiration from an eight-year-old is approximately equal to a standing ovation at Carnegie Hall.

"Can you teach me?" she said.

"Teach you what?"

"How to fix it. So I can do it myself next time."

---

This was how Leela became Aran's student. Not officially. Not in any organized way. She just started showing up.

Tuesdays and Thursdays after school, Leela appeared on the sidewalk with her bike and a question. Sometimes the question was mechanical ("Why does the gear slip?"). Sometimes it was philosophical ("Why do chains break?"). Sometimes it was just a statement dressed as a question ("The back tire is flat and I kicked it and it didn't help?").

Aran taught her what he knew, which was more than he'd thought and less than he'd hoped.

He showed her how to check tire pressure — squeeze the tire, feel for firmness, compare it to a tire that was properly inflated. "Your hands know the difference," he said. "You just have to train them."

He showed her how to oil a chain — a drop every inch, wipe the excess, spin the pedals. "Too much oil collects dirt. Too little and the chain grinds. You want just enough."

He showed her how to adjust the seat height — stand next to the bike, the seat should be at hip level. "A seat too high and you can't touch the ground. Too low and your knees take the work."

Each lesson was small. Five minutes, maybe ten. Leela absorbed it with the ferocity of a kid who wanted to understand things, not just use them.

And something unexpected happened: Aran started understanding things he hadn't understood before.

---

This was the part nobody told you about teaching: it taught the teacher.

When Aran fixed a chain, he did it automatically. His hands knew the motions. His fingers found the sprocket, the chain, the tension — it was body knowledge, lived in the muscles, operating below language.

But when Leela asked "why does the chain go on that way?" he had to go below the automatic. He had to dig into the WHY — the direction of the links, the way the sprocket teeth caught, the physics of rotation and tension that made the whole system work.

"The chain goes on clockwise because the pedals turn clockwise," he explained. "If you put it on the other way, the first pedal stroke would throw it off again."

"How do you know which way is clockwise?"

"Like a clock. The hands go —" He made a circle with his finger.

"What if the clock is digital?"

Aran paused. This was a legitimately good point. Leela was eight. She might never have seen an analog clock. The metaphor his brain had used without thinking — "clockwise" — was built on a reference that might not exist in her world.

"Okay. Forget clockwise. The chain goes on in the direction the pedals push it. When you pedal forward, the top of the chain moves toward the front of the bike. So the chain goes on that way — top forward, bottom backward."

"That makes more sense."

It did make more sense. And Aran realized he'd never thought about it that clearly before. He'd used "clockwise" as a shortcut, a word that pointed at a concept without actually explaining it. Teaching Leela had forced him to throw away the shortcut and build the explanation from scratch.

His understanding of chains was now better than it had been before he taught her. Not because he'd learned something new. Because he'd been forced to translate what he knew into something clear enough for someone else to use.

---

Two weeks in, Leela's chain came off again.

She was riding in front of Aran's house when the familiar snap-scrape happened. She dismounted — more gracefully this time — and looked at the chain. Then she looked at Aran, who was on the porch.

"I can do it," she said.

Aran sat back. "Go ahead."

He watched her crouch next to the bike. Her hands were small and the chain was greasy and the sprocket was awkward at her angle. She reached in, found the chain, and tried to loop it over the teeth.

It slipped. She tried again. Slipped again.

"The trick is —" Aran started.

"I know. Top forward. Let me try."

She tried. Third attempt, the chain caught. She turned the pedals slowly, feeding the links onto the teeth, and the chain settled into place with a satisfying click.

She stood up. Her hands were black with grease. Her face was the face of someone who had done something with their own hands for the first time and was experiencing the particular joy that comes from competence — not praise, not approval, but the quiet, internal knowledge of *I can do this.*

"Got it," she said.

"You got it."

"The trick is going slow. You told me that."

"I told you that because my uncle told me that."

"Who told your uncle?"

"I don't know. Somebody. It goes back. Somebody figured it out and told somebody who told somebody who told my uncle who told me who told you."

Leela considered this chain of transmission — knowledge passed hand to hand, generation to generation, like a chain itself, each link connected to the last.

"So I'm the newest link," she said.

"You're the newest link."

---

At the shop that evening, Aran told Mani about the teaching.

Mani was doing what he always did — working. A brake job this time. Rotors off, calipers hanging, the bare knuckles of the wheel hub exposed.

"She fixed the chain herself," Aran said.

"Third time?"

"Second derailment, third attempt."

"Good. Takes most people four or five."

"I didn't teach her the right way at first. I said 'clockwise' and she didn't know what that meant."

"Because she's eight and clocks are on phones now."

"Exactly. So I had to rebuild the explanation. And the rebuilding made ME understand it better."

Mani looked up from the rotor. "That's the deal."

"What deal?"

"The deal with teaching. You think you're giving something away. But you're actually building something. Every time you explain a thing, you test your own understanding. If the explanation breaks, it's because your understanding had a crack you didn't know about."

"Like the math homework. Knowing the answer versus showing the work."

"Same principle. You knew how to fix a chain. Leela made you show your work. And showing the work made the work stronger."

He held up the rotor — a heavy disc of iron, scored with concentric grooves from thousands of miles of braking.

"See these grooves?"

"Yeah."

"Every groove is a stop. Every stop wore the rotor a little. Over time, the grooves become a record — a history of every time the brakes were used. You can read a rotor like a tree ring. This car stops hard at highway speeds — see the deep grooves? This car stops gently in town — see the shallow ones?"

"What does that have to do with teaching?"

"Teaching leaves grooves. Not on a rotor — on you. Every time you explain something, it cuts a groove in your understanding. Deeper each time. And after enough grooves, the understanding isn't just in your head. It's in your bones."

---

The next Tuesday, Leela showed up with a different question.

"Aran, why do people lie?"

This was not a bicycle question. This was a Tuesday-afternoon ambush disguised as casual conversation.

"Where did that come from?"

"A girl at school said she finished the book report but she didn't. I know because I saw her paper and it was blank. She told Mrs. Davis she'd forgotten it at home."

"Maybe she was scared."

"Of what?"

"Of getting in trouble. Of looking dumb. Of people knowing she hadn't done the work."

"But lying is worse than not doing the work."

"Is it?"

Leela thought about this. She was sitting on the porch steps, her bike leaning against the railing, her chin in her greasy hands.

"Lying makes the problem go away," she said slowly.

"For a while."

"And the truth makes the problem stay."

"For a while."

"So the lie is shorter but the truth is better?"

"I think... the lie is easier right now. The truth is easier later. It depends on which 'now' you're living in."

Leela looked at him with the expression of a person who has just been handed an idea that doesn't fit in their existing mental shelf and needs to build a new shelf for it.

"That's confusing," she said.

"I know. I'm still figuring it out too."

"But you're older."

"Older doesn't mean figured out. Older means more data."

---

That night, in his notebook:

*I've been teaching Leela for three weeks. Bike chains, tire pressure, seat height, and today, why people lie.*

*Here's what I've learned from teaching:*

*1. You don't know something until you can explain it simply. If you need jargon and shortcuts, you're borrowing understanding, not owning it.*

*2. Teaching isn't giving knowledge away. It's testing it. Every explanation is a stress test on your understanding, and the cracks show up when someone asks "but why?"*

*3. Eight-year-olds ask better questions than textbooks. "Why do chains break?" is a better question than "describe the mechanism of chain failure." Same question. Different honesty.*

*4. The chain of teaching goes back. Somebody taught Mani. Mani taught me. I'm teaching Leela. Someday Leela will teach someone. The knowledge moves forward, link by link, and each link adds something — a better explanation, a clearer metaphor, a version that works for a kid who doesn't know what "clockwise" means.*

*5. Leela asked me why people lie, and I didn't have a good answer. I had a partial answer. And the partial answer was more honest than a perfect one would have been, because I'm not done figuring it out. You don't have to be finished to be useful. You just have to be honest about where you are.*

*Uncle Mani says teaching leaves grooves. I think he's right. I can feel the grooves forming. Not in my head — in my understanding. Deeper each time. The things I've taught Leela, I know better now than before I taught them. Not because she taught me back. Because teaching required me to go to the bottom of what I knew and build up from there.*

*Here's the thing nobody tells you about helping someone younger: it doesn't make you feel wise. It makes you feel real. It makes you feel the edges of what you know and the vast spaces of what you don't. And the edges are where the growing happens.*

*Leela fixed the chain herself. She stood up with greasy hands and said "got it." Two words. The weight of a world.*

*She's the newest link. I'm the one before her. And between us, something passes — not just knowledge. Something harder to name.*

*Trust, maybe. The trust of "I know this and I'm giving it to you and I believe you can hold it."*

*That's the best thing I've built all semester. Not the plane. Not the mile time. The look on Leela's face when the chain clicked.*

## Still Thinking?

- Aran says "you don't know something until you can explain it simply." Is that true for you? Think about something you know well. Could you explain it to an eight-year-old? What would you have to change about your explanation?

- The "clockwise" moment — where Aran's shortcut didn't work for Leela — shows that understanding built on shortcuts can break. Can you think of something you "know" that you might not actually be able to explain from scratch?

- Leela asked "why do people lie?" and Aran didn't have a complete answer. He gave a partial one. Is a partial answer better or worse than a confident but incomplete one? When is "I'm still figuring it out" the best thing you can say?

- Aran describes a chain of teaching: somebody taught Mani, Mani taught Aran, Aran teaches Leela. Who taught YOU something important? Do you teach it to anyone else?

- "Helping someone younger doesn't make you feel wise. It makes you feel real." What do you think Aran means by "real" here? Have you ever taught someone something and learned something yourself in the process?
