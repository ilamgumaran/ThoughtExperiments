# Chapter 9: What's Already Here

---

Thanksgiving was weird.

Not bad-weird. Not good-weird. The particular kind of weird that happens when a holiday that used to be one thing becomes another thing, and the space between the two things is filled with an emptiness that nobody mentions.

Last year, Thanksgiving was in Portland. Both parents. One table. The whole family in one house, doing the thing families do — cooking, arguing about cooking, eating, arguing about eating, watching football, arguing about football. Normal. Messy. Complete.

This year, Thanksgiving was split. Thursday with Appa in the new house. Saturday with Amma via video call, which she insisted on even though it was awkward, because she wanted to see Aran's face when he ate the pumpkin pie she'd shipped from her favorite Portland bakery.

Appa was trying. The kitchen was proof. He'd been cooking since 6 AM — a project-managed Thanksgiving, with a spreadsheet on his laptop showing timing for each dish. Turkey at 6:00. Potatoes at 10:00. Green beans at 11:30. Pie warming at 12:00. Each cell color-coded. Each item checked off upon completion.

Mani was coming. Kavi's family was coming — Aran's aunt, uncle, cousins. The house would be full, which was the point. Appa had engineered fullness the way he engineered everything — methodically, with backup plans and contingencies.

But the empty chair was still there. Not literally — Appa had set exactly the right number of places. But the absence of a person leaves a shape, and the shape was Amma-shaped, and Aran could feel it in the room the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue. Constantly. Involuntarily. The tongue goes to the gap. The mind goes to the missing person.

---

The family arrived in waves. First Mani, who came early to help Appa and ended up reorganizing the kitchen in a way that made Appa's spreadsheet irrelevant. Then Kavi's family — Aran's uncle (Appa's brother), his aunt, and cousins Kavi and Meera.

Kavi was eleven now — the same age as Aran — but they were different species of eleven. Kavi was quiet in the way of a person who was busy watching things. Aran was quiet in the way of a person who was busy building things. They got along the way parallel lines get along — moving in the same direction without ever quite touching.

Meera was thirteen and operated at a frequency that Aran found simultaneously impressive and exhausting. She had opinions about everything and expressed them with the confidence of someone who had recently discovered that adults were fallible and was not yet tired of pointing it out.

"The turkey's dry," she announced, after the first bite.

"Meera," her mother said.

"I'm just being honest."

"You're being specific."

"Isn't that the same thing?"

Appa, to his credit, laughed. "It is a little dry. The spreadsheet said three hours but the oven runs hot."

"Maybe cook by temperature, not by time," Mani said, which was a mechanic's solution to a cooking problem and was, annoyingly, exactly right.

Aran ate his turkey. It was a little dry. He didn't mind. The turkey wasn't the point.

---

After the meal, while the adults drank coffee and the cousins scattered, Aran sat on the back porch and looked at the yard.

The yard was small. Smaller than the Portland yard. No tree. No tire swing. No garden patch where Amma had grown tomatoes and basil and the one inexplicable artichoke that she'd nursed all summer and that had produced exactly one edible heart.

This yard had grass and a fence and a view of the neighbor's garage. It was a yard in the way that a room with a bed was a bedroom — technically correct, functionally minimal.

Aran felt the tongue-on-the-gap feeling again. The absence. Not of Amma specifically — he'd talked to her that morning, and she was fine, and he was fine, and "fine" was the word they both used because the real word was too complicated for a phone call. The absence of something larger. The old version of Thanksgiving. The old version of home. The old version of a life that fit together like a model plane — every piece in its place, no extra parts, no missing ones.

That version was gone. This version was here. And "here" was a small yard and a dry turkey and a house full of people who loved him and a chair-shaped space where his mother wasn't.

---

Mani came outside with two cups of chai and handed one to Aran without asking if he wanted it, because Mani understood that sometimes the best way to offer comfort is to not make someone admit they need it.

They sat on the porch steps. The chai was too sweet. Aran drank it anyway.

"You're doing the gap thing," Mani said.

"What gap thing?"

"The thing where you look at what's missing instead of what's here."

"I'm not —"

"I've done it. I do it. Thanksgiving is the worst holiday for it because the whole thing is supposed to be about gratitude, and gratitude is hard when the shape of the table has changed."

Aran held the cup. The warmth spread through his palms, into his fingers, and he realized his hands hadn't been rehearsing. They'd been clenched. Not from anxiety — from holding. Holding on to something that wasn't there anymore.

"I miss her," he said. And saying it — out loud, to Mani, on a porch in the late afternoon — felt like setting something down. Not letting go. Setting down. The way you set a heavy thing on a table because your arms are tired, not because you're done with it.

"I know," Mani said. "She's my sister. I miss her at my own table."

"It's not even that I'm sad. I'm just... aware. Of the gap. Of the thing that used to be there and isn't."

"That's what missing is. It's not sadness. It's awareness of absence. And the awareness can sit right next to everything else — the food, the family, the cousins being ridiculous — without canceling it. You can hold the gap and the fullness at the same time."

"Can you?"

"You're doing it right now."

Aran looked at the chai. At the yard. At the window into the kitchen, where Appa was washing dishes and Kavi was drying them and Meera was supervising in a way that involved a lot of pointing and very little actual labor.

The gap was there. The fullness was there. Both. At the same time.

---

Later, when the cousins were in the living room and the adults were doing the particular kind of talking that happens after the second cup of coffee — looser, warmer, more honest — Aran went for a walk.

Not a run. A walk. The kind where you don't time yourself and you don't track the distance and you just move through the world at the speed of noticing.

He noticed: the streetlights coming on, one by one, in a pattern that moved east to west, like a wave of light following the sunset. He noticed: a family in the house on the corner, visible through their dining room window, still at the table, someone telling a story, someone laughing. He noticed: a dog tied to a porch railing, waiting with the infinite patience of a being that has no concept of time and therefore cannot be made late.

He noticed: the air was cold. The sky was clear. The stars were starting to show — faint, then brighter, then undeniable, as if the darkness was a curtain and the stars were the thing behind it, always there, visible only when the louder light stepped aside.

*What's already here.*

The phrase arrived without announcement. Not from a conversation with Mani or a lesson from Mr. Tanaka. From the walk itself. From the noticing.

The stars were already there. They didn't appear at night — they were revealed at night. They were there during the day too, behind the blue, behind the sun's brightness, invisible only because something louder was in front of them.

What else was already here, hidden behind something louder?

Appa's cooking. Not gourmet, not Instagram-worthy, slightly dry. But present. Every day. Without fail. A man who got up and made dinner because that was how he said "I'm here." That was already here.

Mani's shop. Three blocks away. Open every afternoon. The smell of oil and metal and the steady presence of an uncle who talked in mechanic metaphors and never once told Aran how to feel. That was already here.

Jay's friendship. Messy now, adjusted, different since the hard conversation. But still showing up. Still at the lunch table. Still walking home. That was already here.

The morning run. The notebook. The model plane on the shelf. The body that was getting stronger. The mind that was getting clearer. The wobble that told him when he was off-center.

All already here. All present. All doing their work — quiet, unglamorous, invisible — like the oil filter, like the stars, like the always-there that hides behind the loud-and-missing.

---

Back at the house, Aran sat at the kitchen table. The dishes were done. The leftovers were wrapped. Appa was reading on the couch. The house was quiet in the after-Thanksgiving way — the satisfied quiet of a space that had been full and was now resting.

Kavi's family had gone home. Mani had gone home. The table was clear. Aran ran his hand over the surface — smooth, warm from the kitchen light, marked with the faint rings of a hundred cups of tea.

He pulled out his notebook.

*Thanksgiving. The turkey was dry. Meera was right about that. Appa tried. Mani fixed the kitchen. Kavi and I didn't say much but it was okay.*

*The gap was there all day. I don't think the gap goes away. I think the gap is a permanent shape in the room, and I can spend Thanksgiving staring at it or I can spend Thanksgiving noticing what's around it.*

*Here's what I'm not going to do: perform gratitude. I'm not going to make a list of things I'm thankful for because a teacher told me to, and I'm not going to pretend the gap doesn't hurt, and I'm not going to say "I'm grateful for what I have" in a voice that sounds like a greeting card.*

*Here's what I am going to do: notice what's already here.*

*Not what I wish was here. Not what used to be here. What IS here. Right now. In this room. At this table.*

*Appa's reading lamp. The hum of the refrigerator. The leftover smell of turmeric. A body that ran a mile this morning. A notebook that has sixty-seven days of entries. A model plane on a shelf, built one joint at a time. A friend who I had a hard conversation with and who is still my friend.*

*This is not everything. This is not the old version. The old version had more.*

*But this is real. And real is better than more.*

*Uncle Mani says you can hold the gap and the fullness at the same time. I think he's right. I think the gap makes the fullness more visible, the way the dark makes the stars more visible. Not because the stars are brighter. Because there's less in front of them.*

*Happy Thanksgiving, I guess. Not because everything is perfect. Because everything is here.*

He closed the notebook. Turned off the kitchen light. Went to the living room where Appa was reading and sat next to him on the couch without saying anything, because sometimes sitting next to someone is the whole conversation.

Appa glanced at him. Put an arm around his shoulder. Went back to reading.

The gap was there. The warmth was there. The stars were out, invisible behind the ceiling, but there.

Already here.

## Still Thinking?

- Aran feels the gap — the absence of his mother — like "a missing tooth the tongue keeps finding." Have you ever felt the shape of something missing? Not just sadness, but awareness?

- Mani says "you can hold the gap and the fullness at the same time." Is that true for you? Can you feel something missing AND something present at the same time?

- Aran goes for a walk and notices the stars — "always there, visible only when the louder light stepped aside." What's something in your life that's always been there but you only notice when things get quiet?

- Aran says he won't "perform gratitude" — make a list because a teacher told him to. What's the difference between performed gratitude and real gratitude? How can you tell which one you're doing?

- "Real is better than more." What does this mean to you? Is it possible to be genuinely grateful for something that is less than what you used to have?
