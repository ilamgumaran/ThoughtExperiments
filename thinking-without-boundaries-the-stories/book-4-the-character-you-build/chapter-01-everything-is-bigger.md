# Chapter 1: Everything Is Bigger

---

The building had too many doors.

That was Aran's first thought when Appa dropped him off at Lincoln Middle School on a Monday in September. Not "this is exciting" or "I'm nervous" or any of the things the pamphlet said you were supposed to feel on your first day of sixth grade. Just: there are too many doors. And they all look the same. And I don't know which one is mine.

"You've got your schedule?" Appa asked from the driver's seat.

Aran patted his backpack. "Yeah."

"And your lunch?"

"Yeah."

"And you know where —"

"Appa. I've got it."

Appa nodded. He had the face of a man trying to release something he wasn't ready to release. His hands were still on the steering wheel, engine still running, like the car might decide on its own to drive Aran back home.

"Call me if you need anything."

"I will."

"I mean it. Anything."

"I know."

Aran got out of the car. The door closed behind him with a click that felt too final for something that happened thirty times a day. He stood on the sidewalk and looked at the building and the building looked back.

Lincoln Middle was three stories of beige brick with windows that reflected the morning sun like blank eyes. It was four times the size of Ridgemont Elementary, where Aran had spent the last six years knowing every hallway, every water fountain, every crack in the concrete where ants made their highway. He'd known that building the way you know your own house — not by thinking about it, but by living in it until the walls disappeared.

This building hadn't disappeared. It was very much here. Large and visible and full of doors that all looked the same.

---

Inside was worse.

The hallway was a river. Not Dex's kind of river — the easygoing one that Kavi always talked about, the one you could stand next to without falling in. This was a flash flood. Bodies moving in every direction, backpacks swinging, voices bouncing off metal lockers, the sound of shoes on linoleum creating a low roar that Aran felt in his teeth.

He had a schedule. He'd studied it the night before — first period English, room 217, Mrs. Park. Second period math, room 104, Mr. Okonkwo. Third period science, room 310, Mr. Tanaka. Every period a different room. Every room a different floor. Every floor a different universe.

In elementary school, you sat in one room. Your teacher was your teacher. Your desk was your desk. You knew who sat next to you and what the clock looked like and where the pencil sharpener was. Everything was arranged like a model you'd built — each piece in its place, the whole thing making sense.

Middle school was the model thrown in a box and shaken.

Aran found room 217 by following the numbers, which was simple enough except that the numbers weren't in order. Room 215 was next to room 220. Room 217 was around a corner, past a water fountain that didn't work, behind a group of eighth-graders who looked like they'd been born in this building and would die here and possibly haunt it afterward.

He slipped past the eighth-graders. One of them glanced at him the way you glance at a fly — briefly, without interest, before returning to what matters. Aran's stomach clenched. Not fear. Something smaller. The feeling of being insignificant in a place where significance seemed to be the only currency.

---

Mrs. Park's English class had thirty-two desks and twenty-nine students. Aran found a seat in the second row — not the front (too visible) and not the back (too invisible) — and sat down and looked at his hands.

His hands were doing the thing. The thing where they opened and closed without him asking them to, like they were testing their grip on something that wasn't there. He'd noticed this about himself over the summer — when things got overwhelming, his hands rehearsed. They practiced grabbing, holding, squeezing, as if his body was preparing for something physical even though the challenge was entirely in his head.

He pressed his palms flat on the desk. *Stop rehearsing. There's nothing to grab.*

A kid dropped into the seat next to him — dropped, not sat, the way only someone completely at home in their own body could arrive at a chair.

"New?" the kid said.

"Yeah."

"Me too. Well, new to middle school. I went to Crestview Elementary."

"Ridgemont."

"Cool. I'm Jay." He stuck out his hand, which was a strangely adult thing for an eleven-year-old to do. Aran shook it.

"Aran."

"First day's weird, right? Everything's too big."

"Everything is too big."

Jay grinned. It was the grin of someone who found the world amusing rather than threatening. "It shrinks. My sister's in eighth grade. She says by October you stop noticing the size."

"What do you notice instead?"

"She says you start noticing who people are. Not where the rooms are."

Mrs. Park started talking. She had a voice that was calm and organized, the kind of voice that creates structure without demanding obedience. She talked about expectations, and syllabi, and the reading list for the semester. Aran's mind absorbed about sixty percent of it. The other forty percent was still counting doors.

---

Between first and second period, Aran had four minutes to get from room 217 on the second floor to room 104 on the first floor. Four minutes sounded like enough. It was not enough.

The hallway flooded the moment the bell rang — every door opening simultaneously, releasing bodies into a space that couldn't hold them all. Aran was carried by the current. He couldn't walk at his own pace. He couldn't stop. He was part of the river now, and the river had its own speed.

Someone bumped his backpack. Someone stepped on his shoe. A locker slammed with a sound like a gunshot and he flinched, which nobody saw, which was good, because flinching in middle school was apparently a vulnerability you didn't want to display.

He found the stairs. Went down. Room 104 was to the left — or the right — he couldn't remember, and his schedule was in his backpack, which he couldn't open while moving, so he guessed.

Left was wrong. Left was the gym. He could hear basketballs bouncing inside, which was a strange sound at 8:50 in the morning, like the building had a heartbeat and the heartbeat was rubber on hardwood.

He turned around. Found 104. Sat down. Different desk. Different room. Different teacher. Same hands, rehearsing.

---

Lunch was the most complicated part.

In elementary school, you ate lunch in the cafeteria, at your assigned table, with your assigned class. You didn't choose. The system chose for you, and the system was simple, and simplicity was a gift you didn't appreciate until it was gone.

In middle school, the cafeteria was a field without lines. Three hundred kids, no assigned seats, no assigned groups. You walked in with your tray and you stood there and the entire room was a question you had to answer in real time: *Where do I sit?*

Aran stood in the doorway and looked at the field.

There were patterns. He could see them, the way you could see current patterns in a river if you looked long enough. The athletes sat near the windows. The drama kids had claimed a corner by the vending machines. The kids who'd come from the same elementary school clustered together, their familiarity a kind of gravity that pulled them into orbit around each other.

Aran didn't have orbit. He was new. He was unattached. He was a satellite without a planet, drifting through a cafeteria with a tray of food he wasn't hungry for.

"Aran! Over here!"

Jay. At a table near the middle, with two other kids Aran didn't recognize. Jay waved with the enthusiasm of someone who had never in his life worried about where to sit.

Aran walked over. Sat down. The relief was physical — his shoulders dropped, his jaw unclenched, his hands stopped rehearsing.

"This is Marcus and this is Priya," Jay said, pointing at a tall kid with headphones around his neck and a girl with her hair in a braid so precise it looked architectural. "We're all from Crestview."

"Hi," Aran said.

"You look like you just escaped something," Marcus said.

"I escaped room 310. Mr. Tanaka's science class."

"Tanaka," Priya said, and she made a face that communicated entire paragraphs about Mr. Tanaka. "Good luck."

"That bad?"

"Not bad. Just..." She searched for the word. "Exact."

"Exact?"

"He's exact. Everything has to be exact. Your lab reports, your measurements, your answers. He doesn't accept 'close enough.'"

Aran thought about this. In elementary school, "close enough" was often good enough. You got points for effort. You got credit for trying. The system rewarded intention almost as much as execution.

*Exact* was a different kind of demand. And it made something in Aran — something deep and structural, like a foundation beam — hum with a low, complicated frequency that was part anxiety and part something else.

Something that felt, if he was honest, a little like interest.

---

After school, Aran didn't go home. He walked three blocks to Mani's shop.

Mani's Auto Repair was a small building with two bays, a parking lot that held four cars, and a smell that Aran had loved since he was six. Oil, metal, rubber, and the indefinable scent of machines that had been taken apart and put back together so many times that the air itself remembered the process.

Uncle Mani was under a Honda Civic when Aran walked in. Only his legs were visible — thick, oil-stained legs in work boots that had seen better decades.

"That you?" came a voice from under the car.

"Yeah."

"Grab me the 10-mil socket. It's on the bench."

Aran found the socket — a small, silver cylinder among dozens of identical-looking siblings — and handed it under the car. Uncle Mani's hand appeared, took it, and disappeared again. There was a clicking sound, then a grunt, then more clicking.

"How was the first day?" Mani said from under the Civic.

"Big."

"Big how?"

"Everything. The building. The hallways. The schedule. In elementary school I had one teacher. Now I have seven."

"Seven." A pause. More clicking. "That's seven people who all think their thing is the most important thing."

"Exactly."

"You know what that's like? That's like seven different customers dropping off cars at the same time, and all of them saying theirs is urgent."

"What do you do?"

Mani slid out from under the car. He was a wide man with wide hands and a face that looked like it had been designed for a larger head. His eyebrows were his most expressive feature — thick, mobile, capable of communicating entire philosophies through angle alone.

"I do the one in front of me," he said. "Then the next one. Then the next one. They're all urgent to the person who owns the car. But I've only got two hands. So I work the one that's here."

"What if you can't keep up?"

"You can't. Not at first. New mechanics — first year, they're drowning. Everything's faster than they expect. Everything's harder than the manual says. The cars don't cooperate. The tools don't fit right. They go home thinking they're not cut out for it."

"But they are?"

"Most of them. The ones who show up the next day. Because the building doesn't get smaller, Aran. You get bigger."

He wiped his hands on a rag that had been wiped on so many times it was more oil than cloth.

"You don't get bigger by understanding it all on day one. You get bigger by coming back on day two."

---

That night, Aran sat in his room and looked at his schedule. Seven classes. Seven teachers. Seven rooms. Twelve different hallways between them. One lunch period in a cafeteria that felt like a stadium.

He thought about what Mani had said. *The building doesn't get smaller. You get bigger.*

His hands were still doing the thing — opening, closing, testing their grip on air. But now, sitting in his own room, at his own desk, with his own lamp casting its familiar circle of light, the rehearsing felt less like panic and more like preparation. His body wasn't afraid. It was getting ready.

He pulled out a notebook — not a school notebook, a personal one Appa had given him at the start of summer, saying "you might want to write things down this year." Aran hadn't used it yet. The pages were blank and waiting, the way a workbench is blank and waiting before a project begins.

He picked up a pen and wrote:

*Day 1. Everything is bigger. I found all my rooms but I was late to two. The hallways have their own speed and it's not my speed. I sat with Jay at lunch. He seems like the kind of person who makes things easier by being around. Mr. Tanaka is "exact." I don't know what that means yet but I want to find out.*

He paused. Then added:

*Uncle Mani says you don't get bigger by understanding it all on day one. You get bigger by coming back on day two.*

*Tomorrow is day two.*

He closed the notebook and turned off the lamp. The house was quiet — Appa was downstairs watching something on his laptop, the low murmur of dialogue floating up through the floor. The room was dark but not unfamiliar. This room, at least, he knew. This room was built to his scale.

Outside, somewhere, Lincoln Middle School sat in the dark, its three stories of beige brick waiting for tomorrow. It hadn't gotten smaller since this morning. It was exactly the same size. Every door exactly where it was. Every hallway exactly as long.

But Aran, lying in his bed with his hands finally still, felt something he couldn't name yet. Not confidence — that was too strong. Not hope — that was too clean. Something mechanical. Something structural.

The feeling of a foundation being poured. Not the building yet. Not even the frame. Just the ground the building would stand on. The first layer of something that would take time.

He closed his eyes. His hands rested, flat and open, on the blanket.

Day two was coming.

## Still Thinking?

- Think about a time when everything around you felt too big — a new school, a new neighborhood, a room full of strangers. What did your body do? Did your hands move? Did your stomach clench? Did your shoulders tighten?

- Jay says that by October, you stop noticing the size and start noticing the people. Have you experienced this — where a place that felt huge slowly became normal? How long did it take?

- Uncle Mani says "the building doesn't get smaller — you get bigger." What do you think he means? Can you think of a time you got bigger instead of the situation getting smaller?

- Aran noticed that in elementary school, "close enough" was usually good enough. Is there a difference between "close enough" and "exact"? When does the difference matter?

- Aran's hands rehearse — they open and close when he's overwhelmed, like they're preparing for something physical. Does your body have a rehearsal? Something it does when your mind is busy with too much?
