# Chapter 3: The Long Way

---

The model airplane had forty-seven pieces.

Aran knew this because the box said so — "47 precision-cut balsa wood components" — and because he'd laid them all out on his desk like a surgeon preparing for an operation. Wings. Fuselage panels. Tail assembly. Control surfaces. Struts thinner than pencils. Decals that would go on last, if he got that far, which felt uncertain given that the instruction sheet was fourteen pages long and started with "Read all instructions before beginning assembly."

He'd read all instructions. Twice. The first time to understand what he was building. The second time to understand how long it would take.

The box said "Estimated Build Time: 6-8 hours."

Six to eight HOURS. For a thing that fit on a shelf.

Aran looked at the pieces. He looked at the instructions. He looked at the clock. It was 4:15 on a Saturday afternoon, which meant he had the whole evening, theoretically. But six to eight hours wasn't one evening. It was multiple evenings. Maybe a week. Maybe more, if he made mistakes, which the instructions seemed to assume he would — "if the wing spar does not align, carefully disassemble and retry. Do not force."

Do not force. This phrase appeared seven times in the instructions. Seven warnings against the thing his body most wanted to do.

---

He started with the fuselage. Step one: glue panels A1 and A2 along the center seam. Apply thin, even line of glue. Press together. Hold for 60 seconds. Allow to dry for 10 minutes before proceeding.

Sixty seconds of holding. Ten minutes of waiting. For one seam. And the fuselage had twelve seams.

Aran pressed the panels together and counted. One Mississippi. Two Mississippi. His fingers wanted to let go at fifteen. The glue looked set. The bond seemed solid. Why sixty seconds? Why not twenty?

Because the instructions said sixty. And the instructions were written by people who'd built this thing a hundred times and knew exactly how long the glue needed to become something more than wet.

He held for sixty seconds. It felt like sixty hours.

He set the fuselage down to dry and looked at the clock. 4:22. Seven minutes for one step. Forty-six steps to go.

This was going to take forever.

---

"Forever" was a word that Aran had a complicated relationship with.

Things that actually took forever: waiting in the car while Appa ran into the store "for one thing." Standing in line at the DMV (he'd gone with Appa once and it had permanently changed his understanding of time). The last five minutes of any class before lunch.

Things that felt like forever but weren't: a difficult conversation. A hard math problem. The silence after you said something and the other person didn't respond.

The model airplane was in the second category. It wouldn't actually take forever. It would take six to eight hours. The problem was that six to eight hours, spread over multiple evenings, required something Aran wasn't sure he had.

Patience.

Not the kind of patience adults talked about when they said "be patient" — which usually meant "stop annoying me and wait." Real patience. The kind that lived in your hands and your back and your breathing. The kind that meant doing something slowly, on purpose, when every fiber of your body wanted to go fast.

Aran could feel the wanting in his chest. It was like an engine revving in neutral — producing energy, going nowhere, burning fuel for the sake of the noise. His body wanted to assemble the whole thing NOW. To snap pieces together, to skip the drying time, to jump from step 1 to step 40 and have the finished plane in his hands.

But the glue didn't care about his wanting. The glue took ten minutes to dry. Wanting it to dry faster didn't make it dry faster. It just made the waiting harder.

---

He'd bought the kit with birthday money — thirty-two dollars at the hobby shop downtown, where a man with glasses and a beard had said, "That's a good one. Takes patience." The man had emphasized the word "patience" the way a doctor emphasizes "rest" — knowing you won't do it, saying it anyway.

The reason Aran wanted to build it was Mr. Tanaka.

Two weeks into science class, Mr. Tanaka had shown a video about engineering — how planes fly, the physics of lift and drag, the interplay between weight and thrust. It was the first time a class had made Aran's brain light up since the move. Not because the content was hard, but because it was precise. Every force had a counterforce. Every design choice had a consequence. Nothing was accidental. The plane didn't fly because someone wished it would. It flew because someone understood the laws it was subject to and built within them.

"Constraint isn't limitation," Mr. Tanaka had said, in that quiet, exact voice of his. "It's the beginning of design. Every great structure was built inside constraints. Gravity. Material strength. Budget. Time. The question isn't how to avoid constraints. The question is how to build something excellent within them."

Aran had written this down. Not because it was on the test. Because it felt true in a way that went past school.

---

Ten minutes passed. The fuselage seam was dry. He picked it up and tested the bond — solid, clean, no flex. The panels had become one piece, and the one piece was the beginning of a structure.

Step two: attach the cockpit frame. Four struts, each glued at a specific angle, each requiring thirty seconds of holding and five minutes of drying.

He picked up the first strut. It was the width of a matchstick. Fragile. One snap and it was garbage — the kit didn't come with extras.

He applied glue. A thin, even line. Not too much (it would ooze and look sloppy) and not too little (the bond would fail under stress). The instructions said "thin, even line" and the gap between what that meant in words and what it meant in practice was enormous. How thin? How even? The instructions assumed you'd figure it out through the doing.

He pressed the strut to the fuselage. Held it. Counted.

At fifteen seconds, the strut slipped. The glue hadn't set enough to hold the angle, and the strut slid sideways, leaving a smear of adhesive on the fuselage panel.

Aran's chest clenched. The engine revved. His hands wanted to force the strut back into position, to press hard enough to make it stay, to will the thing into compliance.

*Do not force.*

He took a breath. Set the strut down. Wiped the excess glue with a damp cloth, the way the instructions said. Waited for the smear to dry. Reapplied. Tried again.

This time he held the strut with two fingers — one at the base, one at the tip — distributing the pressure so the angle wouldn't shift. He counted to thirty. The strut held.

One strut. Three minutes of work. Twenty-three seconds of which were productive. The rest was waiting, breathing, and not forcing.

---

Sunday afternoon, Aran was at Mani's shop.

Mani was rebuilding a carburetor — an old one, from a 1987 Ford truck that a customer loved too much to junk. The carburetor was laid out on the workbench in pieces, a constellation of tiny jets and needles and gaskets, each one filthy with decades of fuel residue.

Mani was cleaning each piece individually. A brush. Solvent. Patience. Each jet held up to the light, inspected, cleaned again if necessary. The process was glacial.

"How long does that take?" Aran asked.

"This? Two, three hours for the cleaning. Another hour for the rebuild. Half hour for the tune."

"For one part?"

"For one part that controls how air and fuel mix in the engine. Get this wrong, the whole truck runs rough. Or doesn't run at all."

"Can't you just replace it?"

"With what? They don't make this one anymore. This truck is older than you. The parts are what they are. You work with what you've got."

Aran watched Mani clean a jet — a piece of brass the size of a grain of rice, with a hole through the center so small you could barely see it. Mani held it up to the light, squinting, turning it slowly.

"See that? That hole is the exact size it needs to be. Microns matter. Too big and the engine runs rich — floods, wastes fuel, fouls the plugs. Too small and it runs lean — overheats, loses power, eventually burns a valve."

"How do you know if it's right?"

"You clean it, you measure it, you test it. And if it's not right, you do it again."

"What if you don't have the patience for that?"

Mani set the jet down and looked at Aran with the particular expression of a man who has been asked a question he considers fundamental.

"Then you don't rebuild carburetors."

He said it without judgment. Like stating the weather. The sky is blue. Water is wet. If you don't have the patience, you don't rebuild carburetors.

"But patience isn't..." Aran searched for the words. "It's not something I have. It's not like height or eye color. It's not built in."

"Who told you it was?"

"Nobody. I just assumed. Some people are patient. Some people aren't. You're patient."

Mani laughed — a short, hard bark of amusement. "Kid, I am not a patient man. Ask your aunt. Ask anyone who's known me more than a week. I have the patience of a lit fuse."

"But you're sitting there cleaning tiny brass pieces one at a time."

"That's not patience. That's practice. I've cleaned ten thousand of these. The first hundred, I hated every second. I rushed. I skipped steps. I put carburetors back together with dirty jets and they ran like garbage and I had to take them apart again. And THAT took longer than doing it right the first time."

"So patience is just... doing it badly enough times that you learn to do it slowly?"

"Patience is the thing you build when you stop fighting the time it takes. I'm not patient with this job because I was born calm. I'm patient with this job because I got tired of doing it twice."

---

By Wednesday evening, the model plane was half done.

The fuselage was complete — twelve seams, all solid, no gaps. The wing spars were glued and drying. The tail assembly was laid out, waiting for its turn. Aran had worked on it every evening, thirty to forty-five minutes at a time, and each session ended the same way: with him wanting to do more and the glue telling him to stop.

The waiting was the hardest part. Not because nothing was happening during the waiting — something was happening. The glue was setting. The bond was forming. Chemistry was taking place at a molecular level, polymers linking, adhesion building, the invisible work of becoming strong.

But Aran couldn't see it. And the part of his brain that needed visible progress — the part that wanted to hold the finished thing, to see the whole picture, to skip from here to done — that part was loud. It spoke in the voice of impatience, and impatience, Aran was discovering, felt exactly like hunger. A hollow, pulling, grabbing feeling in his gut that said *more, now, faster*.

He was learning to sit with the hunger. Not feed it. Not fight it. Just sit with it the way you sit with an ache — acknowledging it, letting it be there, not making it the boss.

Thursday evening, he glued the wing to the fuselage. It was the most delicate step so far — the wing had to sit at exactly the right angle (the instructions called it "the angle of incidence") and any deviation would make the plane look wrong and, according to the physics Mr. Tanaka had taught, fly wrong too. If planes were real at this scale. Which they weren't. But the principle stood.

He held the wing in place. Sixty seconds. He counted. His hands were steady — steadier than they'd been on day one. The rehearsing was gone. His fingers weren't grasping at air anymore. They were holding something real, at a specific angle, for a specific time, with a specific purpose.

*This is what patience feels like in the body,* he thought. *Not the absence of wanting. The presence of holding.*

---

Saturday, with the plane nearly done, Aran brought it to the shop to show Mani.

Mani was under a different car — there was always a different car — but he slid out when Aran came in. He wiped his hands, took the plane, and turned it slowly, examining it the way he examined jets: close, careful, with respect for the work.

"You built this?"

"Yeah. Took a week."

"The seams are clean."

"I went slow."

"The wing angle is good. Did you measure it?"

"I used a protractor. The instructions said three degrees."

Mani nodded. He ran a finger along the fuselage — a single, gentle pass, testing the surface.

"The thing about building," he said, "is that nobody sees the waiting. They see the finished plane. They don't see the ten minutes between each glue joint. They don't see the sixty seconds of holding. They don't see the time you wanted to go fast and chose to go slow."

"I know."

"But you see it. You know the ten minutes are in there. Every joint has ten minutes of your time baked into it. And that's what makes it solid. Not the glue. The time."

He handed the plane back. Aran held it, feeling its weight — lighter than he expected, but substantial. A thing that existed because he'd chosen, forty-seven times, to wait instead of rush.

"When I was your age," Mani said, "I broke everything I built. Not because I was careless. Because I was fast. I wanted the thing in my hands more than I wanted the thing to be right. And fast things break. Every time."

"What changed?"

"I got tired of broken things."

---

That night, Aran put the model plane on the shelf above his desk. It sat there — small, precise, real — and he looked at it while he wrote in his notebook.

*The plane took nine evenings. Each evening was thirty to forty-five minutes. Total time: maybe six hours. Which is inside the 6-8 hour estimate, which means the instructions were right, which means the people who wrote them knew something I didn't.*

*They knew that the time isn't an obstacle. The time is the thing. The glue needs ten minutes because ten minutes is what bonding takes. Not seven. Not twelve. Ten. And wanting it to be five doesn't make it five. It just makes you miserable for ten minutes instead of patient for ten minutes.*

*Uncle Mani says patience isn't a trait. It's practice. It's the thing you build when you stop fighting the time it takes.*

*I think he's right. Because I'm not a patient person. I wanted to rush every step. I wanted to skip the drying. I wanted the finished plane in my hands NOW.*

*But I waited. Not because I'm good at waiting. Because I've seen what rushing does. The strut that slipped. The smear of glue. The things that break because you wanted them done more than you wanted them right.*

*The plane is on my shelf. It's not perfect. The left wing is slightly higher than the right — maybe half a degree off. You'd have to measure it to notice.*

*But every joint is solid. Every seam is clean. Every angle is close to what it should be.*

*And the time is in there. Nine evenings of time, invisible, baked into the balsa wood and the glue and the space between steps.*

*That's what patience looks like from the outside: nothing. A kid sitting at a desk, holding two pieces of wood together, counting to sixty.*

*That's what patience feels like from the inside: everything.*

He closed the notebook. The plane caught the light from his desk lamp — a warm, amber glow on the balsa wood, turning it golden. Small. Precise. Built by a boy who wanted to go fast and chose to go slow.

Not once. Forty-seven times.

## Still Thinking?

- Aran says patience feels like hunger — a hollow, pulling feeling that says "more, now, faster." Does patience have a feeling in your body? Where do you feel it?

- Uncle Mani says "patience isn't a trait — it's practice." Do you agree? Can you think of something you're more patient with now than you used to be? What changed?

- The model plane instructions say "do not force" seven times. Is there something in your life right now that you're trying to force? What would it look like to stop forcing and start waiting?

- Aran says "nobody sees the waiting — they see the finished plane." Think about something you've made or accomplished. What invisible time is baked into it that other people don't see?

- Mr. Tanaka says "constraint isn't limitation — it's the beginning of design." What does this mean to you? Can you think of a time when a rule or a limit actually helped you make something better?
