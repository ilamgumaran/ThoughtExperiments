# Chapter 2: The Easy Lie

---

The math homework was supposed to take thirty minutes.

Mr. Okonkwo had said this at the end of class, handing out the worksheet with the confidence of a man who had been assigning the same problems for fifteen years and believed, genuinely, that thirty minutes was a fair estimate. "These are review problems," he'd said. "Things you already know. This should be straightforward."

Aran was sitting at his desk at home, forty-five minutes in, and it was not straightforward.

The problems weren't hard, exactly. He recognized the concepts — fractions, converting between mixed numbers and improper fractions, basic operations. He'd done this in fifth grade. He'd gotten Bs. Solid, respectable, "Aran understands the material" Bs.

But the worksheet was asking him to show his work. Not just the answer. The process. Every step, laid out in order, like a recipe that couldn't skip from "preheat oven" to "serve."

Aran knew the answers. He could look at 3/4 + 5/8 and feel the answer in his head the way you feel the weight of a wrench — intuitively, from practice. But he couldn't explain how he got there. The steps lived in a place below language, in the part of his brain that just *did* things without narrating them.

"Show your work" was asking him to narrate a process he'd automated. It was like being asked to explain how he walked. *I move my right leg forward. I shift my weight. I move my left leg.* He could do it, but by the time he wrote it all down, the natural rhythm was gone and the whole thing felt stilted and wrong.

He stared at problem seven. He'd been staring at problem seven for five minutes. He knew the answer was 2 and 3/16. He did not know how to show the four intermediate steps that would prove he hadn't guessed.

His phone was on his desk. Face down, because Appa had a rule about phones being face down during homework. But face down didn't mean off. And the internet — the entire, infinite, answer-containing internet — was one flip away.

---

Here's the thing about the easy lie.

It doesn't announce itself. It doesn't walk in wearing a black hat and twirling a mustache. It arrives as a reasonable suggestion, dressed in common sense, speaking in a voice that sounds exactly like your own.

*Just look up the steps,* the voice said. *You already know the answer. You're not cheating — you're just checking your work. You're filling in the steps for something you can already do. It's not dishonest. It's efficient.*

Aran's hand twitched toward the phone. The twitch was interesting. It happened before the decision. His body was already reaching before his mind had agreed. Like the decision had been made in a room he didn't have access to, and his conscious mind was just being informed.

He caught the twitch. Noticed it. Pulled his hand back.

*Okay. But the worksheet is going to take another hour if you do it yourself. And you have science to do after this. And English. And you haven't eaten dinner. And Appa is going to ask if you're done and you're going to say no and he's going to look at you with that face — the face that says "why is this taking so long" even though he doesn't say it.*

The voice was persuasive because it was using real problems. The homework really would take another hour. He really did have other subjects. Appa really would make that face. These were true things arranged in service of a false conclusion.

*You're not cheating. You're surviving.*

Aran flipped the phone over. The screen lit up. His thumb hovered over the browser.

And then he put it back down. Face down. And sat there with the worksheet and the problem and the four intermediate steps he couldn't articulate, and he felt like an idiot, which was uncomfortable, and he also felt something else, which was harder to name.

Something like a wheel, slightly off-center. Wobbling. The wobble was faint — barely there — but he could feel it with every rotation. The wobble of almost doing something he knew wasn't right.

---

He got up and went to the kitchen. Appa was making dal, standing at the stove with a wooden spoon and the focused expression of a man who believed that cooking, like coding, was a series of precise operations executed in the correct order.

"Done?" Appa asked.

"No. Math is taking longer than I thought."

"How much longer?"

"I don't know. The answers are easy but showing the work is hard."

Appa nodded. "That's the difference between knowing and understanding."

"What do you mean?"

"At work, we have junior developers who can make the code work. It runs. It does the thing. But if you ask them WHY it works — which function calls which, what happens if this variable changes, where the logic actually lives — they can't explain it. They know the answer but they don't understand the process."

"So I'm a junior developer?"

"You're a sixth grader who got the answer and wants to skip the explanation. It's the same thing."

"But the explanation takes forever."

Appa stirred the dal. The kitchen smelled like turmeric and cumin, which were the smells of home, of steadiness, of a man who made dinner every night because routine was how he loved.

"It takes less time than you think," Appa said. "It just feels long because it's uncomfortable. Understanding is uncomfortable. Knowing is easy."

Aran went back to his room. He sat down. He looked at problem seven. And he did something that felt physically difficult, like pushing against a wall that was pushing back: he started from scratch. Not from the answer. From the beginning.

3/4 + 5/8.

Step one: find the common denominator. The LCD of 4 and 8 is 8.

Step two: convert. 3/4 = 6/8.

Step three: add. 6/8 + 5/8 = 11/8.

Step four: convert to mixed number. 11/8 = 1 and 3/8.

Wait. 1 and 3/8. Not 2 and 3/16.

He'd been wrong. The answer he'd felt so certain about — the one he'd known intuitively, the one he would have written down without question — was wrong.

He stared at the paper. Then he started laughing. Not because it was funny, but because it was so perfectly, painfully instructive. He'd been about to look up the steps for an answer that was WRONG. He'd been about to cheat his way to a mistake.

---

The next day, Aran went to Mani's shop after school.

Mani was replacing brake pads on a Toyota Camry. The old pads were worn down to metal — smooth, shiny metal where there should have been friction material. Mani held one up so Aran could see.

"See this?"

"It's worn out."

"Know what happens when you drive on worn brake pads?"

"You can't stop?"

"You can stop. For a while. The metal still presses against the rotor. You still slow down. But there's no friction material left, so you're grinding metal on metal. Sounds terrible. Feels terrible. Works, kind of, for a while. And then one day it doesn't work, and you're going forty through an intersection wondering what happened."

"What does this have to do with —"

"Nothing. Or everything. Depends on how you look at it." Mani started fitting the new pads. His hands moved with the certainty of repetition — each motion practiced a thousand times until the practice disappeared and only the motion remained. "What's on your mind?"

Aran told him about the math homework. The easy lie. The phone. The wobble. The wrong answer.

Mani listened without looking up. This was how Mani listened — with his hands and his ears, simultaneously, as if attention was a thing that could be divided and multiplied at the same time.

"The wrong answer part is funny," Mani said.

"That's what I thought."

"But that's not the important part."

"What's the important part?"

"The wobble. You said you felt a wobble."

"Yeah. Like a wheel that's not quite true."

"That's your alignment, kid." Mani tapped the brake caliper. "Every car has alignment. The wheels are supposed to point straight, same angle, same direction. When the alignment's off, the car still drives. You can still get where you're going. But the steering pulls. The tires wear unevenly. The whole thing works harder than it needs to."

"And the wobble is the alignment being off?"

"The wobble is you noticing that the alignment is off. Most people don't notice. They just keep driving. The steering pulls left and they pull right to compensate, and after a while they forget the car isn't supposed to do that. They think pulling right is just how driving works."

"So the wobble is... good?"

"The wobble is information. It's your chassis telling you something's not lined up. Whether it's good depends on what you do with it."

He slid the caliper back into place and tightened the bolts. One, two, three, each one to the same torque. Not guessing. Not feeling. Measuring.

"The easy way works for a while," Mani said. "Like metal on metal. It slows you down. But there's no material there. And when you need to stop — really stop, at a moment that matters — you're grinding on nothing."

---

That week, Aran noticed the easy lie everywhere.

Not just in himself — everywhere. It was like learning a new word and then hearing it in every conversation.

In science, a kid named Derek copied the lab measurements from the group next to him instead of running his own trial. Mr. Tanaka didn't catch it. Derek got full marks. No wobble — at least, none visible from the outside.

In English, Mrs. Park asked if everyone had read the assigned chapter. Twenty-nine hands went up. Aran had read it. He was pretty sure at least eight of the twenty-nine hadn't. He could see it in their faces — the particular blankness of someone who'd skimmed the summary online and was hoping the class discussion would fill in the gaps.

In the cafeteria, Jay told a story about his weekend that Aran was ninety percent sure hadn't happened the way Jay described it. Not a big lie. A decorative lie. The kind that makes a story better, funnier, more worth telling. The kind that nobody calls out because everyone does it and calling it out would be like pointing out that the sky is blue — true, but unhelpful.

And Aran himself, on Wednesday, told Appa he'd finished his science reading when he still had two pages left. Two pages. Four minutes of reading. It wasn't even a real lie — it was barely a rounding error. He finished the pages before bed. No harm done.

But the wobble was there. Faint, persistent, the kind of thing you felt in your hands more than your head. His body knew. His body always knew.

---

Friday after school, at the shop, Aran was helping Mani organize a parts shelf that had devolved into chaos over the weeks. Bolts mixed with washers, brake fluid next to transmission fluid, a spark plug in the socket tray for reasons nobody could explain.

"Uncle Mani, do you ever lie?"

Mani was sorting bolts by size, dropping them into labeled bins with a rhythm that was almost musical. Clink. Clink. Clink.

"Everybody lies."

"But do you?"

"Sure. 'How are you?' 'Fine.' That's a lie most days. 'This'll be ready by Thursday.' Sometimes that's a lie. 'That's a reasonable estimate.' That's a lie about half the time."

"Those are small lies."

"They are."

"Do you ever lie about the big stuff?"

Mani stopped sorting. He looked at Aran with an expression that was partly amused and partly something deeper — the look of a man recognizing a question he'd asked himself once, a long time ago.

"I used to. When I was starting out, a customer brought in a car with a transmission problem. I didn't know transmissions yet — I was trained on brakes and engines. But I needed the work. So I said I could fix it."

"Could you?"

"I figured it out. Took me three times longer than it should have. Cost me money in parts I ordered wrong. The customer was unhappy because it took so long. And the fix held for about six months before it failed and he had to take it to a real transmission shop."

"So the lie worked."

"The lie worked for six months. Then reality showed up. Reality always shows up, Aran. It's just a question of when."

He went back to sorting. Clink. Clink. Clink.

"The small lies are practice for the big ones. Not because small lies turn into big lies — not like that. But because every time you take the shortcut and it works, your brain files it under 'this is how things work.' And after a while, when a big moment comes and you need to choose between the hard truth and the easy lie, your brain has a whole filing cabinet of evidence that says the easy lie works fine."

"But the wobble —"

"The wobble gets quieter. That's the dangerous part. The first time, you feel it. The tenth time, you barely notice. The hundredth time, it's gone. And then you're driving on metal, and you don't even remember what brake pads feel like."

---

That night, Aran lay in bed and thought about filing cabinets.

His brain had one. He could feel it — not literally, but the way you feel a truth settling into place, like a bolt tightened to the right torque. The filing cabinet contained every time he'd taken the easy road and gotten away with it. Every "I'm fine" that wasn't fine. Every "I finished" that wasn't finished. Every small, smooth, friction-free shortcut that slid past without consequence.

None of them were catastrophic. None of them had blown up. None of them had even been noticed.

But the cabinet was getting full. And each file was a tiny permission — permission to skip, to skim, to round up, to round down, to call something done that wasn't done.

He picked up his notebook and wrote:

*The easy lie isn't one thing. It's a direction. Every time you take it, you're pointed a little more that way. And after a while, "that way" is just the way you go.*

*The wobble is your alignment telling you something. It's not guilt. It's not shame. It's information. Like a dashboard light. It comes on when something's off.*

*I felt it this week. With the math homework, with the two pages of science reading, with a dozen tiny moments where the easy version was right there and the hard version felt like it was uphill.*

*I don't think the point is to never lie. Uncle Mani lies. Appa lies. Everyone lies. The point is to notice the wobble when it's still small enough to feel. Because Mani says it gets quieter. And when it goes quiet, you're not aligned anymore — you just can't tell.*

He closed the notebook. Outside, a car drove past, its headlights sweeping across the ceiling like a searchlight. Aran watched the light move and thought about alignment.

A car doesn't know its alignment is off. It takes a mechanic to check, a machine to measure, a standard to compare against. But a person — a person has the wobble. The wobble is the machine and the mechanic and the standard, all in one. Built into the body. Available for free.

All you have to do is not ignore it.

He turned off the lamp. His hands were still. Not rehearsing, not grabbing — just resting, palms up, on the blanket.

The wobble was quiet tonight. Not because it was gone. Because there was nothing to wobble about. He'd done his homework — all of it, every step shown. He'd told the truth about what he'd finished and what he hadn't. He'd sorted bolts into labeled bins and put things where they belonged.

Small things. Nothing anyone would notice. Nothing that would make a headline or win an award or get him any kind of credit.

But his alignment was straight. And that, he was learning, was its own kind of feeling. Not pride, exactly. Not satisfaction. Something quieter. Something structural.

The feeling of a wheel running true.

## Still Thinking?

- Aran's body started reaching for the phone before his mind decided. Has something like that happened to you — where your hand (or your mouth, or your feet) moved before you chose to move it? What was the "easy lie" your body was reaching for?

- Aran got the math answer wrong. He was about to cheat his way to a mistake. Have you ever been really sure about something and turned out to be wrong? How did it feel?

- Uncle Mani says the wobble gets quieter over time — that the hundredth small lie barely registers. Do you think that's true? Is there something you used to feel bad about that you've gotten used to?

- "The easy lie isn't one thing. It's a direction." What do you think Aran means by this? Can a direction be changed once you've been going that way for a while?

- Aran noticed the easy lie everywhere — in Derek, in the class, in Jay's story, in himself. Look around your own life this week. Where do you see the easy lie? Not to judge it — just to notice it.
