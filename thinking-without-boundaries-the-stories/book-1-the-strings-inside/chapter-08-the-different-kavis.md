# Chapter 8: The Different Kavis

---

Kavi was four different people, and he hadn't noticed until a Tuesday in November.

It started at breakfast. Appa was in one of his fixing moods — the kind where everything was a problem that needed a solution and Appa was the solution and you were the problem.

"Your teacher emailed," Appa said, reading his phone over coffee. "She says you're doing well in art but you need to participate more in class discussions."

Kavi's stomach clenched. Not because of the email — because of what was about to happen next. He could feel Appa building toward a speech.

"You know, Kavi, speaking up is important. In my job, the people who speak up are the people who get ahead. You can't just sit quietly and expect people to know what you're thinking."

"I know, Appa."

"Do you? Because your teacher says —"

"I know."

Kavi heard his own voice and it was the Appa-Voice. Small. Agreeing. Trying to end the conversation by giving Appa what he needed — acknowledgment that the problem existed, agreement that Kavi would work on it, a nod that said "you're right" even when Kavi wasn't sure he was.

The Appa-Kavi was quiet, compliant, and slightly smaller than his actual body. He sat up straight because Appa noticed posture. He made eye contact because Appa valued it. He said "I know" because it was the fastest way through.

This wasn't lying, exactly. But it wasn't truth either. It was a shape he fit himself into, automatically, the way water fills a glass.

---

An hour later, on the bus, Kavi was someone else entirely.

Dex was telling a story about his dad accidentally buying a fish tank filter that was too powerful and blasting all the gravel out of the tank, and Kavi was laughing — really laughing, the kind that made his stomach hurt and his eyes water.

"— and the fish was just STARING at him, like 'dude, you ruined my house' —"

"Did the fish die?"

"No, the fish was fine! But my dad was on his knees in the bathroom scooping gravel out of the overflow and my mom was like 'I told you to get the small one' —"

The Dex-Kavi was loud. Funny. He made jokes. He interrupted. He said things he hadn't planned on saying and they came out right. His body was loose and open and his voice was bigger and his chest string was barely humming — just a background warmth, the engine running smooth.

Kavi liked this version of himself. The Dex-Kavi felt the most like what "real" might mean. But he couldn't summon it on command. It only showed up around Dex, or sometimes around Thatha, or occasionally alone in his room when he was drawing something that worked.

---

In Ms. Leona's class, there was a third Kavi.

The Classroom-Kavi was watchful. Observing. He knew things he didn't say — answers to questions he didn't raise his hand for, observations about other kids he kept to himself. This Kavi was strategic in a way that felt both smart and dishonest. He calculated risk before speaking. He weighed whether the answer was worth the exposure.

*If I raise my hand and I'm wrong, Rohan will notice. If I'm right, nobody cares. The reward isn't worth the risk.*

This wasn't shyness. Kavi was starting to understand that. Shyness was a label — a sign on the door. What was actually happening was more complicated: the Classroom-Kavi was running a constant social simulation, predicting outcomes, avoiding the ones that activated the spotlight.

It was exhausting. But it was also automatic. The Classroom-Kavi had been doing this for so long that it didn't feel like a choice. It felt like who he was.

---

The fourth Kavi appeared that evening, and he was the one that scared Kavi the most.

Meera and Appa were arguing. This happened more often now — Meera was twelve and had opinions about everything and Appa had opinions about Meera's opinions and the two of them would go back and forth across the dinner table like tennis.

Tonight it was about Meera's phone.

"You don't need to be on it until midnight," Appa said.

"I wasn't on it until midnight. I was on it until eleven."

"That's still too late."

"According to who?"

"According to me. I'm your father."

"That's not a reason. That's just authority."

Amma was doing the thing where she ate faster, hoping to finish before the argument peaked. Thatha was invisible — he had a way of being in the room without being in the conversation, like a plant that nobody addresses.

Kavi sat in his chair and felt the fourth version of himself emerge. The Family-Kavi. This Kavi was still. Very still. He shrank himself down — not physically, but in a way that felt physical — so that the argument would pass over him like weather over a flat field.

The Family-Kavi didn't have opinions. He didn't take sides. He watched the strings — not his own, but everyone else's. He could feel Appa's strings (authority, worry, the need to be respected). He could feel Meera's strings (independence, frustration, the feeling of being treated like a child). He could feel Amma's strings (keep the peace, protect both sides, just get through dinner).

He could feel all of them, and he held them like someone holding too many glasses at once — carefully, barely breathing, afraid that one wrong move would make everything crash.

This Kavi was a mirror. Whatever the room needed, he became. And he'd been doing it so long that he barely noticed anymore.

---

That night, Kavi lay in bed and counted the Kavis.

**Appa-Kavi:** Small, agreeable, eye-contact Kavi. Shaped by the need to end the conversation.

**Dex-Kavi:** Loud, funny, loose Kavi. Shaped by safety — Dex's river carrying him.

**Classroom-Kavi:** Watchful, calculating Kavi. Shaped by the spotlight simulation.

**Family-Kavi:** Invisible, absorbing Kavi. Shaped by the need to hold everyone's strings at once.

Four Kavis. Four different people. In one body.

Which one was real?

The question hit him like the moment you miss a stair — a sudden drop, a lurch in the stomach, the feeling of the ground not being where you expected it to be.

*Which one is real?*

---

He couldn't ask Thatha. Not this time. This one felt too big and too fragile, like carrying a glass full to the brim. Any movement might spill it.

Instead, he lay awake and watched the ceiling fan. Smudge. Gone. Smudge. Gone.

The head string was working overtime: *Maybe the Dex-Kavi is real and the rest are fake. But then am I lying to my dad? Maybe the Appa-Kavi is real and the Dex-Kavi is just performing. But the Dex-Kavi doesn't feel like a performance. Maybe none of them are real. Maybe there's no real Kavi. Maybe I'm just a bunch of shapes and the shape depends on the glass.*

This was terrifying. And also — in a way that Kavi couldn't explain — freeing. Because if none of the shapes were the Real Kavi, then none of them were lies either. They were all... responses. Like how the same ocean makes different shapes depending on whether it hits rocks or sand or a seawall.

Same water. Different shapes.

But that raised another question: if the shapes aren't him — if the Appa-Kavi and the Dex-Kavi and the Classroom-Kavi and the Family-Kavi are just responses to different rooms — then what's the water?

What's the part that stays the same no matter what shape the glass is?

---

He went downstairs at 10 PM. Thatha was on the couch reading, because Thatha kept the hours of a person who had decided that sleep was optional.

"Can I sit?" Kavi said.

Thatha moved his book. Kavi sat.

They were quiet for a while. The house was making its night sounds — Appa's phone charger buzzing, the heater clicking, Meera's music through two walls.

"Thatha, am I different people?"

"What makes you ask?"

Kavi told him. All four Kavis. The shapes and the glass. The water metaphor. The question he couldn't answer.

Thatha listened the way he always did — not just with his ears but with his whole body, turned toward Kavi, completely still, like the listening was its own kind of action.

"You noticed all of that today?" he said.

"I think I've always known. I just never looked at it."

"What does it feel like? To see the different versions?"

"Scary. Because I don't know which one to be. And also... a little bit like relief? Like I've been carrying all these costumes and pretending each one was my real face, and now I know they're costumes, and I can put them down."

"Can you? Put them down?"

Kavi thought about it. "Not all the way. Like, if I'm with Appa tomorrow, the Appa-Kavi will show up. I can feel it coming before it happens — like a string pulling me into that shape. I don't decide to be small and agreeable. I just... become it."

"So the shapes are automatic."

"Yeah. Like the strings. They happen before I choose."

"But you noticed them."

"Yeah."

"Before today, did you notice them?"

"No."

"So what changed?"

Kavi sat with this. What had changed? He hadn't done anything special. He'd just... paid attention. The same way he'd paid attention to the morning hum, and the strings, and the spotlight. He'd looked at something that was always there and actually seen it.

"I looked," Kavi said.

"And now you can see four Kavis."

"Yeah."

"Are there only four?"

Kavi opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"No," he said slowly. "There are probably more. Like, there's the Drawing-Kavi, who's different from all of them. And there's the Porch-Kavi — the one I am with you. And there's a Kavi that shows up when I'm alone, who's different from all the others."

"So many," Thatha said, not mockingly. Wonderingly.

"So which one is me?"

Thatha put his book down entirely. "Let me ask you something. When you're watching all of these Kavis — the Appa one, the Dex one, the classroom one — who's watching?"

Silence.

"I am," Kavi said.

"Which Kavi?"

"I... none of them. Just... me. The one who sees them all."

"And is that one shaped like a glass?"

Kavi felt something shift. Not in his body — somewhere deeper, or maybe wider, like a lens adjusting.

"No," he whispered. "That one doesn't have a shape."

"Does it have a string?"

"No."

"Does it change when you're with Appa versus Dex?"

"No. It's the same. It's always the same. It's the one that's been watching the whole time."

Thatha smiled. The warmest one. The one that meant he wasn't teaching — he was just happy that Kavi had found something on his own.

"That one," Thatha said. "That's the water."

---

Kavi went to bed and didn't draw anything. He didn't need to. The picture was already there, and it wasn't a picture he could put on paper because it wasn't a shape.

All the Kavis were shapes. The water wasn't.

The strings pulled the shapes around — small here, loud there, invisible here, present there. The shapes responded to the glasses, to the rooms, to the people. That was natural. That was how bodies and brains and social situations worked. None of it was wrong.

But underneath all the shapes — the one who watched the shapes, who noticed them, who tracked the strings, who found the quiet between the drips — that one was always the same.

That one didn't need a sign on the door.

---

The next morning, Kavi walked into the kitchen for breakfast. Appa was there, reading his phone. Meera was there, eating cereal. Amma was pouring coffee.

The Appa-Kavi started to form — the shoulders pulling inward, the voice getting smaller, the compliance loading up like a program booting.

But this time, Kavi noticed it forming. Not after. During.

He felt the glass coming, and before it finished shaping him, he paused. Not dramatically. Not in a way anyone would see. Just a tiny internal pause — a breath between the pull and the shape.

"Morning," Kavi said.

It came out as his own voice. Not the Appa-voice. Not the Dex-voice. Not any of the costumes. Just a voice — slightly morning-rough, ordinary, belonging to nobody in particular.

Appa looked up. "Morning. Eat your breakfast."

Nothing had changed. Everything had changed.

## Still Thinking?

- Kavi found four versions of himself for four different situations. How many versions of you can you count? Who are you with your parents? With your best friend? In class? Online? Alone?

- "Same water, different shapes." Does that make sense to you? Is there a part of you that stays the same no matter where you are or who you're with?

- Kavi says the shapes are "automatic — they happen before I choose." Have you ever noticed yourself becoming a different version without deciding to? What triggered the change?

- Thatha asks: "When you're watching all of these Kavis — who's watching?" Think about that. When you notice yourself being a certain way, who's the one noticing?

- The next morning, Kavi pauses between the pull and the shape. He doesn't try to be different — he just notices the shape forming. Do you think that tiny pause changes anything? Could noticing be enough?
