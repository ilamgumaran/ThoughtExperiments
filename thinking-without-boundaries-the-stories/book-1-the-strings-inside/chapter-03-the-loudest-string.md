# Chapter 3: The Loudest String

---

The thing nobody tells you about anger is that it feels good.

Not the after part — the after part is terrible. The after part is when your face is still hot and your hands are still shaking but the thing that made you angry is over, and now you're just standing there with all this fire and nowhere to put it. The after part is when you realize everyone is looking at you. The after part is when Ms. Leona says, "Kavi, can I see you in the hallway?"

But the during part — the actual flash, the moment when anger first shows up — it feels like power. Like your body suddenly knows exactly what to do, even if what it wants to do is wrong. Every other string goes quiet when anger speaks. It's the loudest string, and for one terrible, perfect second, it's the only one.

Kavi learned this on a Thursday.

---

It started with a project.

Ms. Leona assigned group work — "Solar System Posters," which should have been harmless, except she put Kavi in a group with Rohan, Dex, and a quiet girl named Amy. Dex was excited because Dex was always excited. Amy started drawing the sun immediately because Amy was efficient. Kavi was ready to do his part.

Rohan leaned back in his chair.

"I'll supervise," Rohan said.

"That's not a job," Kavi said.

"It is if you're good at it."

Kavi let it go. The chest string tugged — a 3, maybe a 4 — but he'd been practicing. *Notice it. Don't follow it.* He turned to his poster board and started on Saturn's rings.

Things were fine for about fifteen minutes. Kavi drew. Dex colored with the enthusiasm of someone who'd just discovered markers existed. Amy labeled the planets in neat handwriting. Rohan supervised, which meant he leaned back and occasionally said things like "Mercury looks weird" and "that's not the right shade of blue for Neptune."

Then Rohan reached over and drew a smiley face on Jupiter.

"Dude," Dex said, laughing.

"It's funny," Rohan said. "Jupiter is happy."

Kavi looked at the smiley face. It was drawn in thick black marker, right over the storm bands he'd spent ten minutes getting right. The Great Red Spot now had a grin through it.

The chest string went from 4 to 9 in one heartbeat.

"That's my section," Kavi said.

"It's everyone's section. It's group work."

"You haven't done anything."

"I supervised. I'm supervising right now. And I've decided Jupiter needs a face."

The stomach string joined in. Not clenching this time — burning. Like someone had lit a match inside his abdomen. His hands didn't go tingly. They went rigid. He could feel every finger pressing into the desk.

And then Rohan said the thing.

"Dude, relax. It's not that serious. Why do you always take everything so seriously?"

---

Here's what happened inside Kavi in the next three seconds:

**Second 1:** The head string exploded. Not one thought — a flood. *He ruined it. He always does this. Nobody stops him. Ms. Leona doesn't see. It's not fair. I worked on that for ten minutes. He didn't do anything. And now he's telling ME to relax?*

**Second 2:** The chest string overtook everything. It wasn't a pull anymore — it was a push. Outward. Like his ribs were trying to break open. His jaw locked. His vision narrowed. Rohan's face became the only thing in the room.

**Second 3:** The quiet strings — the ones that usually said *be careful, people are watching, this isn't worth it* — went completely silent. Anger doesn't share. When anger speaks, everything else shuts up and sits down.

Kavi stood up fast enough to knock his chair back. It hit the desk behind him with a crack that made the whole class turn.

"YOU DON'T GET TO RUIN SOMETHING AND THEN TELL ME NOT TO CARE."

The cafeteria moment — the one where the fork-dropping kid's face changed before the fork landed — Kavi felt it happen to the whole room. Twenty-six kids' bodies shifted. Chairs creaked. Breath held. The room's strings all pulled the same direction: toward him.

Rohan's face changed. Not to scared. To something else — a blankness, like a wall coming down. Like he'd been here before and had a practiced way of going nowhere.

"Kavi." Ms. Leona's voice, from across the room. Calm. Firm. "Hallway. Now."

---

The hallway was empty and bright. Kavi stood against the wall of cubbies and shook.

Not from cold. Not from crying. From all of it leaving. The anger was draining out like water from a bathtub, and what was left behind was... scattered. The chest was hollow. The stomach was queasy. His hands were trembling, not from rage anymore but from the absence of it. Like his body had been clenched so tight that relaxing felt worse than holding on.

Ms. Leona came out. She didn't look angry. She looked like Amma at the hospital — professional concern, the kind you learn.

"You want to tell me what happened?"

Kavi opened his mouth and the wrong thing came out first: "He drew on Jupiter."

Even as he said it, he heard how it sounded. *He drew on Jupiter.* Like a five-year-old reporting a crime to the playground monitor. Ms. Leona didn't laugh — she was too professional for that — but Kavi could feel her trying not to.

"Okay. He drew on your part of the poster."

"He didn't do any work. He just sat there making comments. And then he ruined the part I worked on. And then he told me I was being too serious."

"And that made you angry."

"It made me —" Kavi stopped. What had it made him? Angry, yes. But under the anger, something else. Something that the anger was standing in front of, like a bodyguard blocking a door.

"It wasn't fair," he said. And his voice cracked, which made him furious at his voice for cracking.

Ms. Leona nodded. Not the "I'm processing this" nod. The "I know" nod.

"It wasn't fair," she agreed. "Rohan should have contributed. And drawing on your work without asking was wrong."

Kavi felt something release. Just hearing someone say it was real — that it wasn't just him being "too serious" — loosened something in his chest.

"But," she said, and he knew the but was coming, "knocking your chair over and yelling isn't something we can do in this classroom. Even when we're right."

"I know."

"Do you? Because in the moment, it didn't seem like you knew."

That was the thing. He did know. He knew it right now, standing in the quiet hallway with the fluorescent lights buzzing. He knew it like he knew his multiplication tables. But three minutes ago, standing in the classroom with Jupiter's ruined face staring at him and Rohan telling him to relax — he didn't know anything. Anger had known. Anger had taken over and done its thing, and Kavi had just been along for the ride.

"It was like I wasn't driving," Kavi said quietly.

Ms. Leona tilted her head. "What do you mean?"

"Like... something else took over. It was so fast. I was sitting there and I was fine — not fine, but okay — and then he said that thing about being serious, and it was like everything went red and the next thing I knew, I was standing and yelling."

"That's actually really honest, Kavi."

"But I couldn't stop it."

"You couldn't stop it THIS time."

She said it like that meant something — like there was a difference between this time and next time — and Kavi wanted to believe her, but his body still felt like a house after a storm.

---

Kavi didn't sit on the porch that evening. He lay on his bed and stared at the ceiling fan. Smudge. Gone. Smudge. Gone.

The anger was fully gone now, and what was left was embarrassment layered over confusion layered over a question he couldn't quite form. He pulled out his sketchbook and looked at his drawing of the gingerbread boy with strings.

The chest string. The stomach string. The hand strings. The head string.

Today, one string had gotten so loud that it drowned out all the others. Like a fire alarm going off — you can't hear the music, the conversation, the birds outside. You can only hear the alarm.

Kavi drew a new picture. Same gingerbread boy. But this time, one string — the chest one — was thick and red and vibrating, and all the other strings were limp, disconnected, silent.

That's what anger was. The loudest string.

He wrote under it: *When the loudest string pulls, everything else stops.*

---

There was a knock on his door. Not Amma (she'd already come in, given him a look that was 70% concern and 30% "we'll talk about this," and left). Not Meera (she'd walked past his door, paused, and kept walking, which was Meera's version of respect).

"Kavi?" Thatha's voice.

"Yeah."

Thatha came in and sat on the edge of the bed. He didn't say anything for a while. Just sat. The Thatha way.

"I heard about school," he said eventually.

"Amma told you?"

"Meera told me. In great detail. She seemed impressed."

Kavi almost smiled. Almost.

"The string was so loud," he said, because he could say this to Thatha without explaining what strings meant. "It was like nothing else existed."

Thatha nodded. "Was there a moment — even a tiny one — where you felt it start? Before it took over?"

Kavi thought about it. Really thought about it, rewinding the afternoon like a video.

Rohan drawing on Jupiter. Strings at a 4. Then 5. Then Rohan saying "why do you always take everything so seriously." And then—

"Yeah," Kavi said slowly. "There was a moment. When he said the thing about being serious. There was this... flash. Like a spark. Right before the fire."

"What was the spark made of?"

Kavi stared at the ceiling. The smudge went around.

"It wasn't just that he ruined the drawing. It was that he said I was too serious for caring. Like caring was wrong. Like I was broken for wanting it to be good."

"So the string wasn't about Jupiter."

Kavi blinked. "What?"

"You said the spark came when he said you were too serious. Not when he drew on the poster."

The room was quiet. The fan hummed.

"The poster was annoying," Kavi said slowly. "But the thing about being serious... that was about ME. He was saying something about me."

"And the string?"

"The string was protecting me. From... from being the kid who's too much. Too serious. Too sensitive. Too everything."

Thatha didn't say anything. He let the words sit in the room like they needed space to breathe.

"The loudest string," Kavi said, "isn't the most important one. It's just the one that thinks it's protecting something."

Thatha put his hand on Kavi's shoulder. Not a pat. Not a squeeze. Just a hand, resting there, like an anchor.

"That's a big thing to see, Kavi."

"It doesn't help. I still yelled."

"You still yelled. And tomorrow, the string will be loud again. Probably about something else. But you saw the spark this time. You saw it after it happened, yes — but you SAW it."

"Thatha, what's the second thing?"

Thatha stood up. "Still the first thing. But you're getting closer."

He left. Kavi lay in bed with his sketchbook open, looking at the gingerbread boy with the angry red string.

He picked up his pencil and drew something small next to the spark: a tiny eye. Open. Watching.

The string was still there. But so was the eye.

## Still Thinking?

- Kavi says anger "feels good" — not the after part, but the flash. Have you ever felt that? Why do you think something that causes trouble can feel powerful in the moment?

- Rohan drew on Jupiter, but that wasn't what really set Kavi off. It was being told he was "too serious." Why do you think that hurt more than the ruined drawing?

- Ms. Leona said "you couldn't stop it THIS time" — like there might be a next time where he could. Do you believe that? What would need to be different?

- Kavi realized the loudest string "thinks it's protecting something." What do you think it was trying to protect?

- Have you ever had a moment where you looked back and saw a "spark" — the tiny moment before a big reaction? What was the spark about?
