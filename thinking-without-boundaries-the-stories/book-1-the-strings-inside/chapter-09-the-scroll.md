# Chapter 9: The Scroll

---

Kavi didn't have a phone.

He was nine, and Amma and Appa had agreed — one of the rare things they agreed on without argument — that ten was the earliest he'd get one. Meera had gotten hers at ten, and the rule was the rule.

This meant that Kavi experienced the scroll secondhand. He watched other people do it the way a kid at the zoo watches fish in a tank — from the other side of the glass, seeing the movement but not understanding what it felt like to swim.

Until Amma left her iPad on the couch one Saturday.

---

It wasn't on purpose. Amma had been scrolling recipes before she left for the hospital, and the iPad sat there with its screen still glowing, open to something with pictures of food and a sidebar of suggested videos. Kavi picked it up intending to bring it to the charger.

He did not bring it to the charger.

The first video was about a dog that could open doors. It was twelve seconds long. The dog pushed a lever handle with its nose, walked through the door, and the video ended. Kavi smiled. Below it was another video: a man making a sandwich in very fast motion. Eleven seconds. Below that: a cat falling off a table in slow motion, set to dramatic music. Nine seconds.

Each video ended and the next one began, and Kavi's thumb — which had apparently learned to scroll without being taught — kept the river flowing. Video. Scroll. Video. Scroll. Video.

He checked the time after what felt like five minutes. It had been forty.

---

Kavi put the iPad down and his body did something strange.

The strings didn't come back gradually. They slammed back like the sound when you take off headphones — sudden, disorienting, too much. The room was too quiet. His hands felt empty. His eyes, used to the bright moving rectangle, drifted around the living room and found it unbearably still. The couch. The bookshelf. The window. Nothing was moving. Nothing was changing.

He wanted to pick the iPad up again.

The wanting was physical. Chest: pulling. Hands: reaching. Stomach: a specific kind of emptiness that felt exactly like hunger but wasn't in the right place. Head: nothing. That was the weird part — the head string was quiet. During those forty minutes of scrolling, the head string had been perfectly, blissfully silent. No worries. No what-ifs. No planning. Just the next thing, and the next thing, and the next thing.

And now that it was over, the head string didn't just come back. It came back loud. Every thought it hadn't been allowed to think during those forty minutes arrived at once, like a dam breaking: *I should do my homework. Did I feed the fish? Why did I waste forty minutes? Amma's going to know I used her iPad. What was that dog video? I want to see it again. I should put this down. I want to pick it up.*

Kavi sat on the couch and felt the pull of the iPad like gravity. It was right there. One arm's length away. The screen was still on. The next video was already queued, a thumbnail promising something funny, something quick, something that would make the head string shut up again.

He understood, suddenly and with complete clarity, what the scroll was.

It wasn't entertainment. It wasn't fun. It wasn't even interesting — he couldn't remember a single video from the forty minutes. Not one. They'd gone through his brain like water through a sieve, leaving nothing behind except the urge to pour more water.

The scroll was a string-silencer.

---

"It turns off the strings," Kavi told Dex on Monday.

They were eating lunch. Dex had his own phone — he'd gotten it at nine because Dex's parents operated on different rules — and it was face-up on the table, screen flickering with notifications.

"What turns off the strings?"

"Scrolling. The phone. I used my mom's iPad on Saturday and I scrolled for forty minutes and I couldn't remember any of it. But while I was doing it, my brain was completely quiet."

"Yeah, dude. That's the whole point."

"But the quiet isn't real. It's not like the quiet between the drips, or the quiet I find on the porch. That quiet feels full. This quiet feels like being turned off."

Dex picked up his phone, looked at it, put it back down. "I don't know. When I'm scrolling, I'm just vibing."

"Okay, but what happens when you stop?"

Dex frowned. "I feel kind of... bleh. For a minute. Then I do something else."

"That bleh. What is it?"

"I don't know. Like, the world is boring for a second. Like nothing real can compete with —" He pointed at his phone. "It's fast in there. Everything's fast. And then you stop and everything is slow and it's like your eyes have to adjust."

"Like coming inside from bright sunlight."

"Yeah. Exactly like that."

---

Kavi started watching people and their phones the way he'd watched the cafeteria during the Spotlight Experiment. And what he saw scared him a little.

**On the bus:** Every kid over ten was looking at a screen. Not all at once — they'd look up, look around, and look back down within three seconds. The look-up was perfunctory, like a swimmer coming up for air before going back under. Their bodies in the real world were still — shoulders hunched, necks bent, fingers moving — while their attention was somewhere else entirely.

**At school:** Between every class, a wave of phones appeared. Five minutes of hallway walking meant five minutes of scrolling, and Kavi could see the same face on every kid — the scroll face. Blank. Slightly glazed. Mouth slightly open. Not happy, not sad. Just... receiving.

**At home:** Meera. Meera was the clearest example because Kavi could watch her for hours. Meera with her phone was a different person than Meera without it. Phone-Meera was still, quiet, absent — a body in a chair with its pilot somewhere else. The moment the phone went away (Amma's rule: no phones at dinner), Real-Meera returned — animated, opinionated, present. But for the first minute after putting the phone down, there was a gap. A transition zone. Meera's eyes would drift. Her fingers would tap the table. Her body was back but her attention was still swimming to the surface.

"You look weird when you come off your phone," Kavi told her.

"Excuse me?"

"For about a minute after you put it down, you look like someone just woke you up."

Meera stared at him. Then — and this was surprising — she didn't deny it.

"Yeah," she said quietly. "It's like that."

"Does it bother you?"

"Sometimes. But then I pick it up again and it stops bothering me."

They looked at each other. The irony didn't need to be spoken.

---

"Thatha, you don't have a phone."

"I have a phone." Thatha pulled it from his pocket — an ancient flip phone with actual buttons. "I just don't have the scroll."

"Why not?"

They were on the porch. Of course they were on the porch.

"Because I watched what it did to your mother."

Kavi blinked. "Amma?"

"Your Amma works twelve-hour shifts. She comes home exhausted. And the first thing she does is sit on the couch and scroll. Not because it gives her energy — because it takes the tired away for a moment. But when she stops, she's more tired than before."

Kavi had seen this. He just hadn't named it.

"It's like a loan," Thatha said. "It borrows peace from the future. While you scroll, everything is quiet. But when you stop, you have to pay it back — with interest. The world feels flatter. Louder. Harder to be in. So you scroll again. And the debt gets bigger."

"That sounds like the moth and the light bulb."

Thatha pointed his tea cup at Kavi. "Exactly like the moth and the light bulb."

"But the light bulb is REALLY bright this time. And it's designed to be bright. Like, the people who make the videos want you to keep scrolling."

"Yes. The light bulb has been engineered."

Kavi sat with that. An engineered light bulb. A string-silencer that you couldn't buy in a store — it came free, in your pocket, and it worked perfectly, and the cost was invisible until you tried to stop.

"So what do you do?" Kavi asked. "When I get a phone — and I will, at ten, Amma already said — what do I do?"

Thatha sipped his tea. "What have you done with every other string?"

"Noticed it."

"And?"

"Watched the pattern. Seen where it pulls."

"And?"

"Not followed it automatically."

"Did you have to cut the other strings to do that?"

"No. They're still there. I just see them now."

"So."

Kavi groaned. "So I'll have a new string. A very bright, very loud, very well-designed string. And the first thing is to see it. Not to fight it. Just to see it."

"Fighting it is another string," Thatha said.

"I KNOW. You always say that."

"Because you keep almost forgetting."

---

Kavi did one more experiment.

He asked Amma if he could use the iPad for twenty minutes. She said yes. He set a timer — twenty minutes, the phone alarm kind that you can't snooze.

Then he scrolled.

But this time, he watched himself scroll. Not the videos — himself. His body. His strings.

**Minute 1:** Strings active. Head string still chattering. Eyes adjusting. The first few videos didn't hit right — too much real-world residue.

**Minute 3:** Transition. The head string went quiet. The chest string relaxed. His breathing slowed. The scroll-rhythm took over — thumb, eyes, thumb, eyes — mechanical, smooth, like a heartbeat he didn't control.

**Minute 7:** Full scroll. No strings. No thoughts. No Kavi, almost. Just the river of content flowing through him. He could feel himself not thinking, the way you can feel yourself not breathing for a moment — it was its own kind of sensation, this absence.

**Minute 12:** A video about a kid drawing caught his attention. Actually caught it — not just held it for three seconds but made him stop scrolling and watch the whole thing. The kid was drawing a bird, and the drawing was beautiful, and Kavi's chest did something different. Not the scroll-nothing. A warmth. The drawing-string.

**Minute 15:** He realized he'd been scrolling past the drawing video for two minutes. The warmth was gone. Back to the river. His thumb had overridden the moment — the scroll had taken the one genuine thing and buried it in more content.

**Minute 20:** The timer went off. He put the iPad down.

The bleh came. Right on schedule. Flat world. Empty hands. Brain waking up grumpy.

But this time, Kavi had data.

---

In his sketchbook that night, he drew the scroll.

Not a phone or an iPad. The feeling. He drew the river — a flowing strip of small bright rectangles, each one different, each one lasting one second before the next one replaced it. And in the river, floating face-up, eyes open but empty, was the gingerbread boy. All his strings were underwater, limp, dissolved in the current.

Above the river, on the bank, he drew another gingerbread boy — same one, but standing. Strings visible. Some tight, some slack. Eyes open and looking at the one in the river.

Under the picture: *The scroll makes the strings quiet. But quiet isn't the same as free. In the river, you don't feel the strings because you can't feel anything. On the bank, you feel the strings — but you can also feel everything else.*

Then, smaller: *The drawing video made me feel something real. The scroll buried it in three seconds. The river doesn't just silence the bad strings. It silences the good ones too.*

## Still Thinking?

- Kavi says the scroll is a "string-silencer" — it makes internal noise stop, but the silence isn't real. Does that match what you feel when you scroll? Is it peace, or is it more like being turned off?

- After forty minutes of scrolling, Kavi couldn't remember a single video. Have you ever scrolled for a long time and realized you can't remember most of it? What was your brain doing during that time?

- Thatha says scrolling is "a loan — it borrows peace from the future." Think about the last time you had a long screen session. How did you feel while doing it? How did you feel five minutes after stopping?

- During his experiment, Kavi found one video that actually mattered — the kid drawing a bird. But the scroll buried it immediately. Has something real ever gotten lost in the river of content? A conversation, a moment, a thought that deserved more time?

- Kavi draws two versions of himself: one floating in the river with no strings, one standing on the bank with all his strings. Which one is more free? Why?
