# Chapter 6: The Sign on the Door

---

There was a sign on Meera's bedroom door.

It used to say "MEERA'S ROOM — KNOCK FIRST" in purple marker with stars around it. She'd made it in third grade, and it had been there so long that Kavi didn't see it anymore. It was like the scratch on the kitchen table or the squeak in the second stair — part of the house's background, invisible from familiarity.

But on a Tuesday in October, Kavi walked past and the sign was different.

The old sign was gone. In its place was a new one, typed and printed on plain white paper, taped with a single piece of tape:

**M.**

That's it. One letter. One period.

Kavi stood in the hallway and stared at it. Something about the change felt bigger than a piece of paper. The old sign was Meera from before — the Meera who drew stars, who used purple, who wanted the world to know this was her space and please respect it. The new sign was Meera from now — stripped down, one letter, no decoration, like she'd decided that less was more and more was embarrassing.

The chest string tugged. Not about him — about her. A different kind of pull, one he didn't have a name for yet.

---

At dinner that night, Kavi watched Meera.

She was different lately. Not in a big, obvious way — she still argued with Appa about screen time, still rolled her eyes at Amma's jokes, still ate her rice with exactly one spoonful of yogurt mixed in. But underneath the usual Meera, something had shifted.

She was quieter. Not sad-quiet. Editing-quiet. Like she was running everything through a filter before it came out, checking it against something — some standard she'd picked up somewhere that Kavi couldn't see.

"How was school?" Amma asked, the same question she asked every night, the same way, like a song she'd forgotten she was singing.

"Fine," Meera said. The word was a door closing.

"What did you do?"

"Classes. Lunch. Normal stuff."

Appa tried: "Any tests coming up?"

"A few."

Three questions. Three walls. Amma and Appa glanced at each other — the parental telepathy that Kavi pretended not to notice — and moved on. But Kavi kept watching.

Meera's jaw was doing the tight thing. The same tight thing Amma's jaw did in the mornings, except Amma's was busy-tight and Meera's was holding-tight. Like there was something in her mouth she was keeping from getting out.

---

Later, Kavi knocked on the door with the M.

"What."

"Can I come in?"

Silence. Then: "Fine."

Meera was lying on her bed with her phone above her face, scrolling. Her room had changed too — not just the sign. The fairy lights were gone. The collage of magazine cutouts above her desk was gone. The walls were almost bare, save for one poster of a band Kavi didn't recognize and a small framed photo of the family from two years ago.

"Your room looks different," Kavi said.

"I cleaned it up."

"You liked the fairy lights."

"I was eleven."

"You're twelve."

"Exactly."

Kavi sat on the edge of her bed, uninvited, which Meera allowed by not pushing him off. This was a good sign. Violent eviction was always an option.

"What happened to the sign?" he asked.

Meera locked her phone and put it face-down. "What sign?"

"Your door. It used to say 'Meera's Room' with stars. Now it just says M."

"I changed it."

"Why?"

"Because the old one was babyish."

"You made it in third grade. It was supposed to be from when you were a kid."

"I'm not a kid anymore."

The way she said it — flat, certain, final — made Kavi's chest string pull in a direction he didn't expect. Not toward fear or protection. Toward something that felt like loss. Like watching someone walk away from a place they used to love without looking back.

---

Kavi couldn't stop thinking about the sign.

Not because of Meera specifically — because of what it meant about something bigger. Meera had looked at the sign she'd made and decided it didn't fit who she was anymore. She'd replaced it with something minimal, something that said "I'm not that person anymore. I'm this person now."

But who decided who "this person" was?

Kavi sat on the porch — Thatha was inside watching the news, so Kavi was alone — and thought about labels.

He had his own. He'd been collecting them since he was small, the way you collect stickers without meaning to. Some were given by other people:

*Sensitive. Quiet. Serious. Artistic. Shy.*

Some he'd given himself:

*Weird. Too much. The kid who notices things. Different.*

And some were assigned by systems he didn't choose:

*Fourth-grader. Son. Brother. Student. Brown kid in a mostly-not-brown school.*

Each label was like a tiny sign on a tiny door. And behind each door was a set of expectations — things he was supposed to do, feel, say, and be because the label said so.

*Sensitive* meant he was supposed to cry easily (he did, but he hated that the label predicted it).

*Artistic* meant he was supposed to be bad at sports (he was, but he hated that it came as a package deal).

*Shy* meant he was supposed to want to be less shy (he didn't, actually, but nobody asked).

When did these signs get put up? Who put them there? And was he allowed to take them down?

---

"Thatha, who am I?"

This was a weird thing to ask your grandfather on a Wednesday evening, but Thatha didn't seem to think so.

"That's a big question."

"I know. But I was thinking about labels. Like how Meera changed her door sign from 'Meera's Room' to just 'M.' And I started thinking about all the signs on MY door. Like 'sensitive' and 'shy' and 'artistic.' Who put them there?"

"Different people. At different times."

"But are they true?"

Thatha looked at him. "What do you think?"

"I think... some of them are true some of the time. I AM sensitive sometimes. But not always. And the label makes it sound like it's all I am."

"What else are you?"

"I'm funny sometimes. With Dex, I'm funny. And I'm angry sometimes — you saw that. And I'm calm sometimes — like on the porch with you. But nobody puts those on a sign because they don't fit the story."

"What story?"

"The story of who Kavi is. The one everyone agrees on. Sensitive, quiet, artistic. It's like... everyone decided who I am, and now I'm supposed to live inside that."

Thatha set down his tea. This was significant — Thatha putting down his tea meant he was giving something his full weight.

"When I was young," he said, "they called me 'the serious one.' My brothers were all loud — joking, fighting, singing. I was the one who read books and sat quietly. And that became my label. 'The serious one.' And here's what happened: I started choosing to be serious because that's what everyone expected. I stopped telling jokes because serious people don't joke. I stopped playing because serious people don't play."

"But you do joke. You're funny."

"I'm funny NOW. It took me fifty years to take the sign off the door."

"Fifty years?"

"Give or take. The sign was put up when I was eight. I didn't question it until I was nearly sixty. That's a long time to live inside a label someone else made."

---

Kavi went to his room and opened his sketchbook. He drew the gingerbread boy again — he was getting good at this, the proportions were less gingerbread and more human now — but this time he drew something different.

Instead of strings, he drew signs. Little rectangular labels stuck to different parts of the body.

On the head: **Smart / Not Smart Enough**
On the chest: **Sensitive / Too Sensitive**
On the arms: **Artistic / Not Athletic**
On the stomach: **Brave / Actually Scared**
On the legs: **Shy / Wants to Be Left Alone**

Each sign had two sides. The label, and what the label didn't say.

And then he drew something that surprised him: he drew a hand reaching up to one of the signs — the "Sensitive" one on the chest — and peeling it off. Not throwing it away. Just holding it. Looking at it. Like it was a sticker that could be removed, examined, and maybe put back if it still fit, or set aside if it didn't.

*The signs aren't me,* he wrote. *They're ON me. And signs can be moved.*

---

The next day at school, something happened that made the whole thing click.

Ms. Leona was doing a get-to-know-you activity — "Write three words that describe you on this index card." Kavi had done activities like this a hundred times. He always wrote: Quiet. Artistic. Nice.

This time, he held the card and his pencil and didn't write anything.

The head string said: *Just write the usual things. That's what they expect.*

But the question underneath the question was: are those words me, or are they the signs on my door?

Dex, next to him, wrote his in two seconds: "LOUD. FAST. HUNGRY." He showed Kavi and grinned. Kavi smiled, but he noticed something: Dex's three words were true, but they were also a performance. Dex was loud and fast and hungry, yes. But Dex was also the kid who checked on Kavi when he looked sad. Dex was the kid who cried during the movie about the dog in second grade and denied it for a year. Dex was kind, and scared sometimes, and quieter than people thought.

But "Kind. Scared sometimes. Quieter than people think" doesn't fit on a sign. It's too complicated. It's too true.

Kavi wrote on his card: **Still figuring it out.**

Ms. Leona collected the cards. She didn't read them out loud (thank God). But later, when she was handing back worksheets, she paused at Kavi's desk and said, quietly, "That was a really good answer."

---

On the porch that evening, Kavi said: "I think the labels are another kind of string."

Thatha raised an eyebrow. "How so?"

"The strings pull me toward feelings — fear, anger, worry. But labels pull me toward a version of myself. They say 'you are this' and then my body arranges itself to match. Like, if I believe I'm shy, then when I walk into a room, my strings pull toward shy behavior. The label came first. The feeling followed."

"So the sign on the door changes what happens inside the room."

"YES. That's exactly it."

"And if you take the sign down?"

"I don't know yet. I think... the room is still there. I'm still in it. But I get to look around and see what's actually in there instead of just what the sign says should be in there."

Thatha picked up his tea. Took a long, slow sip. The kind of sip that was really a pause.

"You know," he said, "when I finally took my sign down — 'the serious one' — I didn't become unserious. I'm still serious. I still read books and sit quietly. But I also laugh. I also play with you. I also dance badly when the cricket match is on the radio, which your Amma has unfortunately witnessed."

"So you're still you."

"I'm still me. But me is bigger than the sign."

The streetlight came on. Moths appeared, doing their light-bulb dance. Kavi thought about the moth from weeks ago — the one that circled and circled the porch light, pulled by something it couldn't understand.

The moth and the light. The string and the pull. The label and the behavior.

All of them were the same thing: something that seems like it's out there — the light, the pull, the expectation — but is actually being powered from in here.

*The sign on the door doesn't describe what's inside,* Kavi thought. *It decides what's inside. Unless you take it down and look for yourself.*

He picked up his sketchbook and drew one more thing: the gingerbread boy, standing in front of a door. The door had a blank space where a sign used to be. And the boy was holding the sign in his hand, reading it, deciding.

Not throwing it away. Just deciding.

---

That night, Kavi walked past Meera's door. The white paper with "M." was still there. He thought about knocking, about telling her what he'd figured out — that the sign wasn't who she was, it was who she was deciding to be, and deciding was different from being.

But he didn't knock. Some things you can't tell someone. They have to walk past their own door and notice their own sign and wonder, on their own, if it still fits.

He went to his room and looked at his own door. It didn't have a sign on it. It never had.

He thought about putting one up. Then he thought: maybe no sign is the best sign.

He went to bed. The strings were quiet. Not because they were gone.

Because, for tonight at least, none of the signs were telling them what to do.

## Still Thinking?

- If you had to put signs on your door — labels that describe you — what would they say? Who put them there — you, your family, your friends, kids at school?

- Kavi noticed that Dex's three words ("Loud, Fast, Hungry") were true but incomplete. If someone described you in three words, what would they get RIGHT, and what would they MISS?

- Thatha was "the serious one" for fifty years. Is there a label you've had for a long time that might be only partly true?

- "The sign on the door doesn't describe what's inside — it decides what's inside." What do you think this means? Can a label actually change how you act?

- Meera changed her sign from "Meera's Room" to "M." Why do you think she did that? Is she taking a label off, or putting a new one on?
