# Chapter 12: The Strings Don't Go Away

---

December came, and with it, Kavi's tenth birthday.

He didn't want a party. He wanted a cake (chocolate, no fondant, because fondant was a lie that pretended to be cake), and he wanted to have dinner with his family, and he wanted Dex to come over and build something afterward. Simple. Small. A birthday shaped like him.

Amma made the cake. Appa brought home balloons, even though Kavi hadn't asked for them, because Appa expressed love through logistics. Meera made him a card — hand-drawn, which she hadn't done in years, with a picture of a gingerbread boy holding a sketchbook. She'd seen his drawings. He didn't know she'd noticed.

"Happy birthday, weird kid," she said, handing it to him.

"Thanks, mean kid."

She smiled. He smiled. A string hummed between them — not a pulling string, a resonating one, like two guitar strings vibrating at the same pitch.

Thatha gave him a new sketchbook. Same kind — black cover, thick pages. But inside the front cover, in Thatha's careful handwriting:

*For the one who watches. Keep looking.*

Kavi read it twice. Then closed the sketchbook and hugged Thatha, which he hadn't done in months, because somewhere between nine and ten he'd started thinking hugging your grandfather was maybe not what you did anymore. The head string had said so.

The hug was better than the head string.

---

After cake, after Dex went home, after the house got quiet, Kavi sat on his bed with the old sketchbook and the new one. He opened the old one to the first page: the gingerbread boy with strings. The one he'd drawn months ago, the first time he'd named the hum.

He flipped through.

The gingerbread boy with the thick red anger string. The boy standing between drips, finding the quiet. The room full of boys with spotlights, each one pointing at themselves. The boy peeling a label off his chest. The river of scrolling rectangles with the boy floating face-up, eyes empty. The filing cabinet full of wrong answers. The blank page with one sentence in the middle.

A whole semester of noticing, drawn in pencil and marker and oil pastel, filling a sketchbook that used to be empty.

Kavi looked at the pictures and waited for the feeling of accomplishment. The feeling of having figured it out. The feeling of being done.

It didn't come.

Because he wasn't done. The strings were still there. All of them.

---

He knew this already, in theory. But December made it real in a way that theory hadn't.

**The morning hum was still there.** Every day, before the alarm, the chest vibrated. Some days it was a 2. Some days, when a test was coming or when Amma and Appa had argued the night before, it was a 6.

**The anger string was still there.** Rohan had said something two weeks ago — something about Kavi "always staring at people like a creep" — and the flash had come, hot and fast. Kavi hadn't yelled this time. He'd felt the spark, felt the chest trying to expand, and said nothing. But the anger lived in his stomach for three hours afterward, circling like a dog that couldn't find a place to lie down.

**The spotlight was still there.** On the bus yesterday, Kavi had tripped getting off and the spotlight blazed — 7, maybe 8 — and his face burned even though nobody laughed. Two kids had actually helped him pick up his folder. But the spotlight didn't care about kindness. It only recorded embarrassment.

**The shame string was still there.** He'd gotten a C on an English essay, and the cold stone had dropped into his stomach, and the head string had whispered *you're not a good writer* even though writing wasn't his thing and a C was fine and none of it mattered in any real way. Three days to fade. Same as before.

**The label strings were still there.** Just last week, a substitute teacher had said "you must be the quiet one" and the label had snapped on like a collar, and for the rest of the period Kavi had performed quiet — hadn't raised his hand once, hadn't spoken to Dex, had been exactly what the label predicted.

**The scroll pull was still there.** He'd gotten a phone for his birthday (Amma and Appa had bumped the timeline — ten was close enough) and the first time he opened a video app, the river was there, waiting, warm and bright and endless.

All of it. Still there. Every string he'd named, every pattern he'd tracked, every pull he'd drawn — still active, still pulling, still doing exactly what strings do.

---

"I thought it would go away," Kavi told Thatha.

He was sitting on the porch with his new phone in his pocket (turned off, for now) and his old sketchbook in his lap. The December air was cold enough for jackets and warm enough for the porch.

"What would go away?"

"The strings. I thought if I saw them — really saw them — they'd stop. Like, understanding the pattern would break the pattern."

"And?"

"They're all still there. The hum. The anger. The shame. The labels. All of it. I can see them now, but they still pull."

Thatha nodded like this was exactly the conversation he'd been waiting for.

"Did I tell you about learning to drive?"

"You can drive?" Kavi had genuinely never seen Thatha drive anything.

"I used to. When I was learning, my instructor said something I never forgot. He said: 'You don't learn to drive by making the road smooth. You learn to drive on the road as it is.'"

"What does that mean?"

"It means the road doesn't change. The potholes don't disappear because you learned where they are. But you stop driving into them."

"But I still drive into them! The anger — I didn't yell at Rohan, but I felt it for three hours. The shame — the C on the essay sat in my stomach for three days. The spotlight still fires every time I trip."

"Three hours. Three days. What was it before?"

Kavi paused.

"The anger used to be... all day. Sometimes two days. And the shame... the 58 lasted a week."

"So?"

"So it's... shorter. But it's not gone."

"Does the sun go away at night?"

"No. It's just... on the other side."

"The strings don't go away, Kavi. You're right about that. But you're wrong that nothing has changed."

---

That night, Kavi did something he hadn't planned. He sat at his desk with both sketchbooks — the full one and the empty one — and drew one final picture in the old book.

The gingerbread boy. One last time. But different from every other version.

This boy had strings. All of them. The chest string, the stomach string, the head string, the hand strings — all present, all attached, some taut and some slack. And the boy also had signs — labels stuck on him, some of them straight and some peeling at the corners. And above him, the spotlight, pointing down.

Everything was there. All the machinery of being a person in a world. Nothing removed. Nothing solved. Nothing fixed.

But the boy's face was different. In every other drawing, the gingerbread boy had been a blank — two dots for eyes, a line for a mouth, an everykid. This time, Kavi drew an actual face. His face, or close to it — messy hair, one ear slightly bigger than the other, eyes that were looking not at the strings or the labels or the spotlight, but outward. At the viewer. At whoever was looking at the drawing.

And the expression on the face was not peaceful. Not solved. Not enlightened. It was the look of a kid who knows what's happening to him and is still standing.

Under the picture, Kavi wrote:

*The strings don't go away.*

*The hum doesn't stop.*

*The anger still flares. The shame still drops. The spotlight still burns. The labels still stick. The scroll still pulls.*

*None of it goes away.*

*But I can see them now.*

*And seeing them doesn't cut the strings. It doesn't silence the hum. It doesn't fix anything, really.*

*What it does is give me one extra second.*

*One second between the string pulling and me following.*

*One second where I'm not the puppet.*

*One second where I get to choose.*

*That's not everything.*

*But it's not nothing either.*

---

He closed the old sketchbook and opened the new one. Blank pages. White space. The same potential that had stopped him before, when he'd realized the blank page was already complete.

But tonight, the blank page wasn't a destination. It was a beginning. The first sketchbook had been about looking inward — finding the strings, the quiet, the shapes, the water. The new sketchbook would be about looking outward — at the world, at other people, at the questions that didn't have neat answers.

Kavi picked up his pencil. He didn't know what he was going to draw. He didn't have a plan. He just put the pencil on the paper and let his hand move, the way the river moved, the way the clouds moved, the way the bee moved between flowers — not because someone told it to, but because moving was what it did.

He drew for an hour. He didn't think about strings. He didn't analyze anything. He just drew.

When he was done, he looked at what he'd made. It was a tree. Not a perfect tree — the proportions were off, the branches were uneven, the leaves were more suggestion than detail. But it was alive. You could feel it. The way the trunk leaned slightly left, the way the branches reached out like arms stretching after sleep, the way the roots gripped the ground like fingers holding on.

It was just a tree. But it was his tree. Made by his hand. In the quiet between the strings.

He turned off the light and got into bed. The morning hum was already there, faintly, preparing for tomorrow. But tonight, just tonight, it sounded less like an alarm and more like a song he was getting to know.

*I'm ten,* Kavi thought. *I've got a lot of strings.*

*Good thing I've got time.*

---

He fell asleep. Outside, the December stars were bright in the cold air. The porch light was off — Thatha had gone to bed for once, his tea cup rinsed and upside down on the counter. Mrs. Henderson's house was dark. Mr. Patel's car sat in his driveway, parked, going nowhere.

The neighborhood slept. The world hummed.

And somewhere inside a ten-year-old boy with messy hair and one ear slightly bigger than the other, the strings were there, and the quiet was there, and both of them were okay, and that was enough.

That was enough.

## Still Thinking?

- Kavi expected that seeing the strings would make them go away. They didn't. Does that feel discouraging to you, or does it feel honest? Why?

- "One second between the string pulling and me following." Think about your own life. Is there a moment — even a tiny one — where you've paused between the pull and the reaction? What happened in that pause?

- Kavi says the one extra second is "not everything, but it's not nothing either." What can you do with one second? Is one second enough?

- Throughout this whole story, nobody taught Kavi a technique or gave him a solution. He just noticed things. Is noticing enough? What does it actually change?

- Kavi opens a new, blank sketchbook. If you had a blank book and a pencil and the beginning of something — not the answer, but the beginning — what would you draw? What would you write?
