# Chapter 5: The Spotlight Experiment

---

On Monday, Kavi wore a new shirt to school.

This sounds like nothing. People wear new shirts all the time. But Kavi was not "people." Kavi was a person who'd spent an entire Sunday evening standing in front of his closet trying to decide if a blue shirt with small dinosaurs on it was cool or not cool, and whether "not cool" was a risk worth taking, and what "cool" even meant when you really thought about it.

The dinosaurs were small. Tasteful, even. Little T-Rexes in a repeating pattern. Amma had bought it because she thought it was fun. Kavi liked it in the store. He liked it at home. He liked it right up until the moment he imagined wearing it in front of other people, and then every string in his body had an opinion.

Chest: *People will look at you.*
Stomach: *What if someone says something?*
Head: *Rohan. Rohan will definitely say something.*
Hands: Fidgeting with the hem, already rehearsing pulling the shirt down or covering it with a jacket.

He wore it anyway, because Thatha happened to walk past his room and see him holding the shirt and said, "That's a good one," and for some reason Thatha saying it made the strings go from an 8 to a 5, which was manageable.

---

Nobody said anything about the shirt.

Not at the bus stop. Not on the bus. Not in homeroom. Not in math. Kavi sat through the entire morning with every string tuned to maximum surveillance — scanning faces, interpreting glances, reading micro-expressions like a tiny FBI agent — and the result was: nothing. Zero comments. Zero looks. Zero dinosaur-related incidents.

By lunch, the strings were exhausted. Kavi was exhausted. He'd spent four hours bracing for an impact that never came, and he was more tired than if someone had actually made fun of him.

"Cool shirt," Dex said at lunch, and moved on to talking about whether hot dogs were sandwiches.

That was it.

---

"I don't get it," Kavi told Meera after school.

He hadn't planned on talking to Meera about this. But she was eating Oreos at the kitchen table and seemed to be in a rare state of not being annoyed, and the question was burning a hole in his chest.

"Don't get what?"

"I wore a new shirt today. I was terrified someone would say something about it. Nobody did. But I was more tired than if they had."

Meera twisted an Oreo apart and scraped the cream with her teeth. "Welcome to my entire life."

"What?"

"Kavi. I spend literally every morning deciding what to wear based on who might say what. I've been doing this since fifth grade."

"But nobody says anything."

"Exactly! Nobody says anything! But my brain doesn't know that. My brain acts like I'm walking into a courtroom and everyone is judging me."

"Why?"

Meera put the empty Oreo halves down and looked at him like he'd asked why the sky was up. "Because that's what brains do. They put you under a spotlight even when there's no spotlight."

---

The spotlight. That was a new word for an old feeling.

Kavi thought about it all evening. The spotlight wasn't a string exactly — it was more like what happened when multiple strings pulled in the same direction. The chest string (something's coming), the stomach string (danger), the head string (people are watching) — all of them together created the feeling that he was on a stage, lit up, visible, and everyone was an audience with opinions.

But here was the thing Meera had accidentally revealed: **the spotlight was his.**

Nobody was actually pointing it at him. Nobody was watching. Everyone was too busy being under their own spotlight to notice his dinosaur shirt or his weird walk or the way he held his pencil.

The spotlight was something his own strings created, and then he believed it was real.

---

"Thatha. I have a theory."

Wednesday evening. Porch. Tea. The usual setup.

"Tell me."

"Okay. So. I think there's this thing — Meera calls it the spotlight. It's the feeling that everyone is looking at you. Like you're the main character and everyone else is the audience."

Thatha sipped his tea. "Go on."

"But nobody is actually looking. I wore a dinosaur shirt and nobody cared. Meera picks her outfit every morning like it's a presidential debate, and nobody cares. Everyone is too busy worrying about their own stuff."

"So the spotlight is..."

"Mine. I'm pointing it at myself. My strings are creating it."

"And what does that tell you?"

Kavi had been thinking about this. "It tells me that a lot of the things I'm afraid of aren't actually happening outside me. They're happening inside me. The strings are reacting to something that my brain made up."

A car drove by. Thatha waved to the driver — Mr. Patel from three houses down, who always drove too slowly.

"Can I tell you something about when I was young?" Thatha said.

Kavi leaned forward. Thatha almost never talked about being young.

"When I was about your age — maybe ten — I had to give a speech at school. In front of the whole class. About a book I'd read."

"What happened?"

"I prepared for two weeks. I wrote the speech. I memorized it. I practiced in front of the mirror. And then I stood up in front of the class and my mind went completely blank."

"What did you do?"

"I stood there. For what felt like an hour but was probably thirty seconds. And in those thirty seconds, I was certain — CERTAIN — that every person in that room was thinking about how stupid I looked."

"Were they?"

"I asked my friend afterward. Do you know what he said?"

"What?"

"He said he hadn't noticed. He'd been busy trying to remember his own speech."

Kavi laughed. Then stopped. "But it felt real."

"It felt completely real. More real than anything. My body — the shaking, the sweat, the blank mind — all of that was real. But the thing causing it — 'everyone is judging me' — that was the spotlight. Mine. Nobody else's."

"So the feelings are real but the reason isn't?"

Thatha set his tea down. "That's a very precise way to put it. Yes. The strings pull for real. Your body reacts for real. But the story your brain tells about WHY they're pulling — that story is often fiction."

---

Kavi decided to run an experiment.

He called it the Spotlight Experiment, and it was simple: for one full day, instead of assuming people were looking at him, he was going to look at them. Not to judge — just to see. Were they actually paying attention to him? Or were they busy with their own stuff?

Thursday. The experiment began.

**Bus, 7:45 AM:** Kavi sat near the front and watched. Twenty-three kids on the bus. Five were on their phones. Three were asleep. Two were arguing about a video game. Six were talking in pairs. The rest were staring out windows or at nothing. Number who looked at Kavi: zero. Number who appeared to be thinking about Kavi: zero.

**Homeroom, 8:10 AM:** Kavi dropped his pencil. It clattered on the floor. His strings went to a 5 — *everyone heard that, everyone's looking.* He looked up. One kid glanced over for half a second and looked away. Everyone else: nothing. Already back to their own lives before the pencil finished rolling.

**Hallway, 10:15 AM:** Kavi walked to the bathroom and counted how many people made eye contact with him. Out of maybe forty kids he passed: three. And all three were the automatic, one-second kind of eye contact that happens when two humans pass each other. Not interest. Not judgment. Just the basic mammal-brain check of "is that a person? yes. moving on."

**Cafeteria, 12:00 PM:** This was the big one. The cafeteria always felt like the spotlight's home base — the place where everyone could see everyone and social positions were on display. Kavi sat in his usual spot and, instead of feeling watched, he watched.

Here's what he saw:

The fifth-grade table was having some kind of crisis — a girl was crying and two friends were leaning in and a boy across the table was pretending not to notice while clearly noticing. The popular kids were doing the thing where they laugh slightly too loud so everyone knows they're having fun. The quiet table by the window had four kids reading — actual books, actual reading — and Kavi had never noticed them before. The lunch ladies were having their own conversation, animated, one of them laughing so hard she had to put down her ladle.

Everyone was in their own movie. Their own spotlight. Their own strings pulling them through their own scene.

Kavi was an extra in everyone else's movie. And they were extras in his.

"What are you looking at?" Dex asked, mouth full of pizza.

"Everything," Kavi said.

"Cool. Want my breadstick?"

---

That night, Kavi drew his most complex picture yet.

Not just one gingerbread boy. A whole room of them. Twenty-six gingerbread figures, each with their own strings pulling in their own directions. Some strings were tangled with neighbors' strings. Some were pulling toward the same thing. But most were completely independent — twenty-six separate puppet shows happening in the same room, each one convinced it was the only show.

And above each figure, he drew a small circle of light. A spotlight. Each one pointing downward at its own figure. Twenty-six spotlights, none of them pointing at anyone else.

He wrote at the bottom: *Everyone has their own spotlight. Nobody is standing in yours.*

Then he crossed it out and wrote something better: *The spotlight is real. The audience isn't.*

---

On Friday, something unexpected happened.

Kavi was wearing the dinosaur shirt again (why not — nobody had noticed the first time), and he was walking to his desk when Rohan said, "Hey. Nice dinosaurs."

Every string pulled. The chest lit up. The stomach clenched. The head started building a defensive speech—

But then Kavi looked at Rohan's face.

Rohan wasn't smirking. He wasn't setting up a punchline. He was just... looking at the shirt with the expression of a kid who thought dinosaurs were kind of cool but would never in a million years admit it unprompted.

"Thanks," Kavi said.

"My little brother is into dinosaurs," Rohan said. "He has like a hundred figurines."

"That's cool."

"Yeah." Rohan looked away. Something moved across his face — a string, Kavi thought, pulling him back to the version of Rohan that didn't admit things — and then it was gone and Rohan was Rohan again.

But Kavi had seen it. Under the bluntness and the jokes and the "I'll supervise" attitude — strings. Different from Kavi's, pulling in different directions, but just as real.

Rohan had a spotlight too. And in that spotlight, he was the kid who couldn't show he cared about anything without it feeling dangerous.

Kavi sat down at his desk and felt something strange: the chest string was quiet. Not because he'd found the quiet between drips or because he'd tried not to look directly. But because, for a second, the spotlight had swung from himself to someone else, and what he'd seen there wasn't an audience.

It was just another kid. Under his own light. Pulling his own strings.

Trying, like everyone else, not to fall.

## Still Thinking?

- Kavi spent four hours bracing for something that never happened (people judging his shirt). Have you ever spent a long time worrying about something that turned out to be nothing? How did it feel after?

- "The feelings are real but the reason isn't." Think about the last time you were nervous about something social. Was the thing you were nervous about actually as dangerous as your body thought it was?

- The Spotlight Experiment: next time you're in a cafeteria, a classroom, or a bus, try watching other people instead of worrying about being watched. What are they actually doing? Are they thinking about you, or about their own stuff?

- Kavi saw that Rohan has strings too — that under the tough act, there's a kid who can't show he cares without it feeling dangerous. Is there someone in your life who acts tough or mean, and you've wondered what might be going on underneath?
