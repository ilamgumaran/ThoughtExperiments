# Chapter 1: The Morning Hum

---

The alarm didn't wake Kavi up. He was already awake.

He'd been lying there for a while, watching the ceiling fan go around. One of the blades had a dark smudge on it — maybe a dead bug, maybe just dirt — and every time it came around, Kavi's eyes found it again. Smudge. Gone. Smudge. Gone. Smudge.

His body was doing something before his brain caught up. There was this hum — not a sound, exactly, but a feeling. Like his insides were vibrating at a frequency he couldn't name. It was in his chest, mostly. Sometimes his stomach. It happened every morning before school, and he'd never told anyone about it because he didn't know what to call it.

It wasn't fear. He wasn't scared of school. It wasn't excitement either. It was just... there. A readiness that nobody asked for.

The alarm went off. He smacked it.

---

Downstairs, Amma was already in motion. She moved through the kitchen like a person solving a puzzle — cereal here, lunchbox there, one eye on her phone where the hospital schedule glowed. She was wearing her scrubs, the blue ones with the tiny planets on them that Kavi had picked out two birthdays ago.

"Eat," she said, not looking up. "Meera, your lunch is by the door. Kavi, shoes."

Kavi wasn't wearing shoes. He wasn't wearing socks either. He was standing in the kitchen doorway, watching.

Here's what he noticed: Amma's jaw was tight. Not angry-tight. Busy-tight. The kind of tight that said *I have seventeen things to do and I'm on number four*. He noticed that her coffee was sitting by the microwave, forgotten, which meant she'd poured it and then got pulled away before drinking it. He noticed that the lunchbox she packed for Meera had the chips on top, which Meera would rearrange because she always ate the sandwich first.

He noticed all of this in about three seconds, and none of it was useful. His brain just... collected things. Like a net dragging through water, catching everything whether he wanted it or not.

"Kavi. Shoes. Now."

He went to find his shoes.

---

On the bus, Dex was already talking.

Dex was always already talking. It was like he'd been mid-sentence since birth and hadn't found a period yet. Today it was about a video he'd seen where a guy built a catapult out of pencils and launched an eraser across a classroom.

"— and it went like FORTY FEET, Kavi, I swear. You think we could build one? We'd need rubber bands. Thick ones. My dad has some in his desk —"

Kavi nodded. He liked Dex. Being around Dex was like standing next to a river — you didn't have to do anything, you just had to not fall in. Dex filled all the space, and that meant Kavi didn't have to.

But the hum was still there. Sitting behind Dex's words like a bassline under a song. Kavi could feel it in his chest, and he put his hand there without thinking about it.

"You okay?" Dex said, noticing.

"Yeah. Stomachache."

It wasn't a stomachache. But "stomachache" was a word that worked, and the thing in his chest didn't have one.

---

School started the way it always did — with sitting.

Ms. Leona's classroom had twenty-six desks arranged in clusters of four, and Kavi's cluster included Dex (good), Priya (fine), and Rohan (complicated). Rohan was the kind of kid who said exactly what he was thinking at exactly the wrong time, and somehow never got in as much trouble as he should have.

"Alright, everyone," Ms. Leona said. She had a way of starting class that made it sound like she was about to share a secret. "Before we start math, I want to try something. I want everyone to close their eyes for thirty seconds."

Twenty-six fourth graders looked at her like she'd asked them to juggle.

"Just thirty seconds. Eyes closed. No talking. I'll time it."

Giggles. Shuffling. Rohan muttered "this is weird" and Dex made an exaggerated meditation pose with his fingers in little circles. Ms. Leona waited with the patience of someone who has done this a thousand times.

"Eyes closed. Starting... now."

Kavi closed his eyes.

The first thing that happened was noise got louder. Not real noise — the buzz of the lights, someone breathing through their mouth two seats over, a chair creaking — but it was like turning up the volume on a room he'd been in all morning without hearing.

The second thing that happened was the hum.

Without his eyes to look at things, the hum filled the space. It was right there. Not painful. Not scary. Just... present. Like it had been waiting for him to stop being busy so it could introduce itself properly.

*Hi. I'm the thing in your chest. I've been here all morning. Actually, I've been here a lot longer than that.*

Kavi almost opened his eyes. Not because he was scared — because it was loud. How had he not noticed how loud it was?

"Ten more seconds," Ms. Leona said.

In those ten seconds, Kavi noticed something he'd never noticed before: the hum wasn't one thing. It was layers. There was the chest one (the main one, the loudest). There was a smaller one in his stomach, faster, like a hummingbird. And there was one in his hands — not a hum exactly, but a tingling, like his fingers wanted to grab something but didn't know what.

"Okay. Eyes open."

Twenty-six pairs of eyes opened. Most kids looked relieved. Dex immediately whispered "that was the longest thirty seconds of my life." Priya looked thoughtful. Rohan looked annoyed, like he'd been tricked into doing something uncool.

Kavi looked at his hands.

---

Ms. Leona asked: "What did you notice?"

This was the kind of question teachers asked when they wanted a specific answer but pretended they didn't. Kavi knew the game — you were supposed to say something like "it was quiet" or "I heard the clock" and then the teacher would nod and steer you toward whatever the lesson was actually about.

"I heard Jacob breathing," someone said.

"Hey!" Jacob said.

"It was boring," said Rohan.

"I felt kind of dizzy," said a girl named Sara.

Ms. Leona nodded at each one like they were all correct, which, Kavi realized, they probably were.

She didn't ask Kavi. But if she had, he wouldn't have known what to say. Because what he'd noticed wasn't something he'd ever been asked about in school. Not in a worksheet. Not in a textbook. Not in any question that had a blank line waiting for the answer.

What he'd noticed was: something is always happening inside me, even when nothing is happening outside.

---

At lunch, Kavi sat with Dex and ate his sandwich (peanut butter, no jelly, the way he liked it) and watched the cafeteria.

Watching the cafeteria was like watching a nature documentary. There were patterns. The fifth graders always took the back corner. The loud kids sat in the middle where everyone could hear them. The quiet kids sat near the windows. Teachers stood at the edges like referees at a game where nobody knew the rules.

Kavi watched a kid across the room drop his fork, and before the fork hit the ground, the kid's face changed. Not just "oops, I dropped my fork" — his whole expression rearranged, like he was checking to see if anyone saw, like dropping a fork was a test he could fail.

The hum in Kavi's chest fluttered.

*That kid felt something before the fork even landed,* Kavi thought. *The same way I feel something before the alarm goes off. Before Ms. Leona calls on someone. Before Rohan says something mean.*

It was always before. The feeling came first. Then the thing happened. Or sometimes the thing didn't happen, and the feeling hung around anyway, embarrassed, like a guest who showed up to a party on the wrong day.

"You going to eat that?" Dex pointed at Kavi's apple slices.

"Yeah." Kavi ate an apple slice. It was sour. His face scrunched.

"Told you the green ones are sour."

"I like sour."

"You literally just made a face."

"I like sour AND making faces."

Dex laughed. This was easy. This was the river, flowing. No hum when he was laughing with Dex. Or — no, the hum was still there. It was just quieter. Like it didn't need to be loud when things were okay.

*Interesting*, Kavi thought. And then he thought: *Wait. Since when do I think "interesting"?*

---

After school, Kavi sat on the porch with Thatha.

Thatha was doing what Thatha always did: sitting. He had a cup of tea that he drank in slow sips, and he watched the street like it was the most fascinating show on television. A squirrel ran across a power line. A car drove by with music playing. Mrs. Henderson across the street watered her flowers.

Kavi sat next to him and didn't say anything for a while. This was the deal with Thatha. You could be quiet and it wasn't weird. With most people, silence felt like a hole you had to fill. With Thatha, silence felt like a chair you could sit in.

"Thatha?"

"Hmm."

"Do you ever feel a thing before the thing happens?"

Thatha took a sip of tea. He didn't answer right away, and Kavi knew this meant he was actually thinking about it, not just waiting for Kavi to explain more.

"What kind of thing?" Thatha said.

"Like... a hum. In your chest. Before something happens. Like your body already knows something is coming, but your brain hasn't caught up yet."

Thatha looked at the street. A bird landed on Mrs. Henderson's mailbox.

"You know how that bird landed?" Thatha said.

Kavi looked. "On the mailbox?"

"Before it landed. Its body was already adjusting. Wings shifting, feet out, angle changing. All of that happened before it touched the mailbox. You think the bird decided all that?"

"I don't think birds decide things."

"So who's flying?"

Kavi frowned. "Its body. Its body just knows."

Thatha took another sip of tea. "Bodies know a lot of things. Ours too."

"But the hum. What IS it?"

Thatha smiled — not the kind of smile that means "how cute," but the kind that means "good question, and I'm not going to ruin it by answering it."

"What do you think it is?" he said.

Kavi opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"I don't know. But it's always there. And today I noticed it's not just one thing. It's like... layers."

"Layers," Thatha repeated, like he was tasting the word.

"Yeah. Chest. Stomach. Hands. All doing different things at the same time."

Thatha nodded slowly. Then he said something that Kavi would think about for a long time:

"Most people stop noticing. You haven't. That's not a problem, Kavi. That's a skill."

The bird flew away. Mrs. Henderson went inside. The street was quiet.

The hum was still there. But for the first time, it didn't feel like something wrong. It felt like something that had been waiting a very long time to be noticed.

## Still Thinking?

- Next time you wake up in the morning, before you get out of bed, try lying still for ten seconds. Can you feel anything happening in your body? Where is it? Is it one thing or layers?

- Think about the last time you were in a room full of people. Did your body feel different than when you were alone? How?

- Kavi noticed the kid who dropped his fork. The kid's face changed before the fork even hit the ground. Why do you think that happened?

- Is there someone in your life — like Thatha is for Kavi — where silence doesn't feel like something you need to fill?
