# Chapter 2: The Invisible Strings

---

The thing about noticing is that once you start, you can't stop.

Kavi discovered this on a Tuesday, which is the most nothing day of the week. Nothing special about Tuesday. No pizza Friday. No Monday dread. Tuesday is just... there. And that's exactly why it was the perfect day for everything to change.

It started in math.

Ms. Leona was explaining fractions — the kind with pictures of pies, which always made Kavi hungry — and Kavi was following along fine until she said, "Now I'm going to call on someone to come solve this on the board."

The hum slammed on like a car horn.

Not gradually. Not a gentle wave. It went from background noise to full blast in the time it took Ms. Leona to scan the room. Kavi's chest squeezed. His hands went cold. His stomach dropped like he was on a roller coaster that he hadn't agreed to ride.

And here's the thing: Ms. Leona hadn't called on him. She hadn't even looked at him. She'd said "someone," and his body had reacted as if she'd pointed directly at his face and said "YOU. NOW."

Kavi gripped the edge of his desk and waited.

"Priya, can you come up?"

Priya walked to the board. Kavi's body released — the chest loosened, the hands warmed up, the stomach climbed back to where it belonged. It was over. Nothing had happened to him. Nothing was ever going to happen to him. But his body had just gone through an entire emergency and come back like it was no big deal.

*What WAS that?* Kavi thought.

---

At recess, Kavi sat on the low wall near the basketball court and watched.

Dex was playing four square with the usual crowd. Kavi sometimes played, but today he was doing something else. He was watching people and trying to catch the moment — the before-moment, the one he'd noticed yesterday — when their bodies did something that their brains hadn't decided yet.

It took about forty seconds.

A fifth-grader named Marco was waiting for his turn. He was standing with his arms crossed and his weight on one foot, looking like he didn't care. But when the kid in the king square caught the ball and looked around choosing who to send it to, Kavi saw Marco's shoulders tighten. Just slightly. Just for a second. Like his body was bracing for something that his face was pretending didn't matter.

The ball went to someone else. Marco's shoulders dropped.

He never uncrossed his arms. He never changed his expression. But his body had done a whole thing — tighten, brace, release — and Kavi bet that if you'd asked Marco what happened, he'd say "nothing."

*It's not just me,* Kavi thought. *Everyone has them.*

---

That night, Kavi tried to draw it.

He sat at his desk with his sketchbook — the one with the black cover that Thatha gave him last Diwali — and tried to draw what he'd been feeling. Not a person. Not a thing. The feeling itself.

He drew himself first. Just a simple outline, the kind where you're basically a gingerbread man. Then he thought about the morning hum and drew a line from his chest going upward, like a string attached to something above him. Then the stomach feeling — another string, this one going downward, pulling toward the floor. Then the hand thing — two strings going outward, reaching for something invisible.

He stared at it. A gingerbread boy with strings coming out of him in four directions, each one pulling somewhere different.

That's what it felt like.

Not one feeling. Not one pull. Multiple strings, each attached to something he couldn't see, each pulling at a different strength and a different speed. Sometimes one was loud and the others were quiet. Sometimes they all pulled at once and his body didn't know which way to go.

He wrote under the drawing, in his messy handwriting: **The Invisible Strings.**

---

The next morning, Kavi tested it.

He lay in bed when the alarm went off and, instead of smacking it, he just... lay there. Like Ms. Leona's thirty seconds, except this time he was looking for something specific.

*Where are the strings right now?*

Chest: There. The morning hum. Not as loud as yesterday. Maybe a 4 out of 10. It was pulling upward and forward, like it wanted him to get out of bed and start dealing with the day.

Stomach: Tighter than the chest. A 5. This one was harder to read — it wasn't pulling in a direction. It was more like... clenching. Like a fist that wasn't sure if it needed to be a fist yet.

Hands: Nothing. They were just hands. Relaxed, warm under the blanket.

Head: Oh. This was new. There was something behind his eyes. Not pain — pressure. Like his brain was already building a list of things that hadn't happened yet. *What's for breakfast. Did I finish my homework. Is it library day. What if Rohan says something mean.*

"Huh," Kavi said out loud.

The string behind his eyes was pulling him into the future — into things that hadn't happened. The chest string was pulling him forward, into action. The stomach was just... on guard.

And all of this was happening while he was lying perfectly still in a perfectly safe bed in a perfectly normal house.

His body was preparing for a battle that didn't exist.

---

"You look weird," Meera said at breakfast.

Meera always said stuff like this. She was twelve and had reached the age where observing other people's flaws was a hobby.

"I'm thinking," Kavi said.

"About what?"

He almost told her. He almost said "invisible strings" and "body feelings" and "the hum," but something stopped him. Not embarrassment — more like the sense that if he put it into words too soon, the words would make it smaller than it was.

"Math," he said.

"You hate math."

"I'm thinking about how much I hate math."

Meera rolled her eyes. But Kavi noticed — because he was noticing everything now, which was becoming kind of exhausting — that Meera had her own thing going on. She was eating her cereal fast. Not hungry-fast. Nervous-fast. Her leg was bouncing under the table, and she kept checking her phone, which was face-down next to her bowl, like she was waiting for it to say something.

*Meera has strings too,* Kavi thought. *Hers are pulling her toward her phone.*

He almost asked her about it. But Amma walked in with her coffee (remembered this time) and the morning routine resumed its usual chaos.

---

At school, Kavi made a decision: he was going to track his strings for one whole day.

Not write them down — Ms. Leona might notice, and he didn't want to explain this to anyone yet. But in his head, he'd keep a running count. Every time he felt a string pull, he'd notice it. That was it. Just notice.

**8:15 AM — Morning announcements.** The principal's voice came over the speaker. Chest string: mild pull (2 out of 10). No reason. Just the sound of authority.

**8:30 AM — Reading time.** All strings quiet. This surprised Kavi. He was reading a book about a kid who builds a raft, and while he was reading, the strings basically disappeared. Not gone — he could still find them if he looked — but they went so quiet it was like they'd fallen asleep.

**9:15 AM — Ms. Leona announces a pop quiz.** Every string in his body lit up at once. Chest: 8. Stomach: 7. Hands: sudden tingling, like electricity. Head: a wall of noise — *I didn't study, what if I fail, does this count for the grade, why didn't she warn us.* He gripped his pencil so hard it left a dent in his finger.

**9:20 AM — The quiz is actually just five questions and they're easy.** Strings slowly released, one by one, like someone unclenching their fingers. But the stomach kept a low hum for the next ten minutes, like it didn't trust that the danger was actually over.

**10:00 AM — Recess.** Playing tag with Dex. Strings quiet again — not like reading-quiet, but like they were busy doing something else. Running made them hard to feel. Or maybe running made them... useful? The chest pull wasn't anxious anymore. It was just energy, pointing at something fun.

**11:30 AM — Rohan says, "Nice shoes, Kavi. Did your mom pick those out?"** Every string pulls at once, but different from the pop quiz. This wasn't fear. This was heat. Stomach: burning. Chest: expanding, like his ribs were trying to push outward. Face: hot. Hands: fists.

Kavi didn't say anything back. Rohan moved on, because that's what Rohan did — he threw rocks and kept walking. But Kavi's strings didn't settle for twenty minutes. Twenty minutes of replaying the comment, thinking of things he should have said, imagining scenarios where he'd been cool about it or mean about it or anything other than silent.

**12:00 PM — Lunch.** Sitting with Dex. Strings at a 3. Mostly the stomach one. He realized: the Rohan strings hadn't gone away. They'd just gone underground. He could still feel them, like a bruise you keep touching.

**2:30 PM — Art class.** Strings went silent. Completely. Not quiet — silent. Kavi was drawing with oil pastels, making a landscape that didn't look like anything real, and it was like someone had cut all the strings at once. His body was just... his body. No pulling. No guessing. No preparing. Just color on paper.

When art ended, the strings came back like someone flipping a switch. Just like that. Quiet to noise in two seconds.

---

"Thatha?" Kavi said that evening on the porch.

"Hmm."

"I tracked them today. The things in my body. All day."

Thatha's eyebrows went up. Not a lot — Thatha's face didn't do a lot — but enough that Kavi could tell he was interested.

"What did you find?"

Kavi told him. All of it. The numbers. The different strings. The way they went quiet during reading and art, the way Rohan's comment made them burn, the way they came back the second art class ended.

Thatha listened without interrupting. When Kavi finished, the street was dark and the porch light had come on, attracting a small cloud of moths.

"So they're not random," Thatha said.

"No! That's what I thought too. They follow... patterns. Like, my chest one is about what's coming next. My stomach one is about danger — like, is something bad going to happen? And my hands one is about wanting to DO something about it."

"And what happened during art?"

"Nothing. They all stopped."

"Nothing was pulling you?"

Kavi thought about this. "No... I was pulling myself. Wait, no. I wasn't pulling at all. I was just doing the thing. I was just drawing."

"And the strings?"

"They didn't need to pull because I was already where I wanted to be."

The moths circled the light. A big one bumped against the bulb, backed away, circled, bumped again.

"That moth," Thatha said, "is being pulled toward the light. Watch what happens."

Kavi watched. The moth kept circling. Keep bumping. Never landing, never leaving. Stuck in a loop of being pulled by something that didn't actually have anything for it.

"Sometimes," Thatha said, "the string is pulling you toward something real. Hunger pulls you toward food. That's useful. But sometimes the string is pulling you toward the light bulb. And you circle and circle and circle."

"How do you know which is which?"

Thatha smiled. The good kind.

"You're already doing it. You watch. You notice. You see the pattern. That's the first thing. The only first thing."

"But what's the second thing?"

"We haven't finished the first thing yet."

Kavi groaned. Thatha laughed — a rare sound, quiet and warm like the tea he always drank.

"You've had these strings your whole life, Kavi. One day of noticing them is not the end of something. It's the beginning."

The moth found the edge of the porch light and rested there, still close to the glow but no longer crashing into it.

Kavi watched it for a long time.

## Still Thinking?

- Kavi tracked his strings for a day. If you did the same thing, what do you think you'd find? Which parts of your day would be loud, and which would be quiet?

- Kavi noticed that during reading and art, his strings went quiet. Is there something you do where your body gets really quiet — where you're just doing the thing without any pulling?

- Rohan's comment about Kavi's shoes was small. But Kavi's body reacted like it was something big. Have you ever had a small thing that felt way bigger than it should have? What was your body doing during it?

- Thatha talks about the moth and the light bulb — being pulled toward something that doesn't have anything for you. Can you think of a time when you kept going back to something that didn't actually help?
