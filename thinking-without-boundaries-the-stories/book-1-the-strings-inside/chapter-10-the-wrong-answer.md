# Chapter 10: The Wrong Answer

---

Kavi got a 58 on his science test.

Not a 58 out of 100 on a big exam — a 58 on a small quiz about rocks. Igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic. Twelve questions. He got five wrong because he'd mixed up metamorphic and sedimentary, which were, in his defense, both very boring types of rocks.

Ms. Leona handed the quizzes back face-down, which she did when some people hadn't done well, so that nobody would see anyone else's score. This was supposed to be a kindness. In practice, it made every quiz feel like a secret shame.

Kavi flipped his over. Saw the 58. And his body did something new.

It wasn't the anger-string. It wasn't the spotlight. It wasn't any string he'd named before. This one started in his face — a flush that rose from his neck to his ears like a tide — and then dropped into his stomach, where it sat like a cold stone. His chest didn't tighten. It caved. Like someone had removed a support beam and the whole structure leaned inward.

The head string said: *You're stupid.*

Not "you got a bad grade." Not "you should have studied." Not any of the reasonable things that a reasonable brain might say. The very first thought, arriving before anything else, was: *You're stupid.*

And the second thought, arriving a quarter-second later, was worse: *Everyone will know.*

---

Nobody knew.

The quizzes were face-down. Ms. Leona didn't announce scores. The kid next to Kavi — Jacob — was too busy being happy about his own grade to notice anyone else's. But Kavi's body didn't care about reality. Kavi's body was living in a world where the 58 was written on his forehead, visible to everyone, a permanent public announcement of his failure.

The shame string — because that's what this was, a string he'd never had to name before — was different from all the others.

Anger was hot and outward. Anger wanted to DO something — yell, push, defend.

Fear was cold and inward. Fear wanted to HIDE — shrink, avoid, become invisible.

Shame was both at once. It was hot in the face and cold in the stomach. It wanted to hide AND defend at the same time, which made it the most confusing string of all — like two people grabbing your arms and pulling in opposite directions.

And underneath the shame, fueling it, was a belief that Kavi hadn't known he carried until this exact moment:

*Getting it wrong means I am wrong.*

---

Dex got a 42.

"Rocks are stupid," Dex said at lunch, eating his pizza with the serenity of a person who had decided that geology's opinion of him was irrelevant.

"You're not upset?" Kavi asked.

"I mean, my mom's gonna be like 'you need to study more,' and I'll be like 'yeah,' and then we'll move on. It's rocks, Kavi. Rocks."

"But doesn't it make you feel... bad? About yourself?"

Dex stopped chewing. This was significant — Dex rarely interrupted a meal for philosophical purposes.

"Bad about myself? No. I'm bad at remembering rock types. That's not the same thing."

"It feels the same."

"It's not, though. Like, I'm also bad at making my bed and bad at remembering to feed my fish. That doesn't make me a bad person. It makes me a person who forgets stuff."

Kavi stared at his pizza. The cold stone in his stomach was still there. He could hear what Dex was saying, and it made logical sense, but the string didn't care about logic. The string said: *58 means stupid. Stupid means broken. Broken means something is fundamentally wrong with you.*

"How do you do that?" Kavi said. "How do you keep the grade separate from the person?"

Dex shrugged. "I dunno. Maybe I just don't think about it that hard."

And there it was — the thing Kavi both admired and envied about Dex. Not that Dex was carefree. That Dex didn't turn every experience into a verdict on his worth. The quiz was a quiz. The grade was a grade. The kid was a kid. Three separate things.

For Kavi, they merged. The quiz became the grade became the kid. A 58 on rocks became a referendum on his value as a human being.

*That's not reasonable,* Kavi thought. *But it's real.*

---

The shame lingered for three days.

Not at the same intensity — it faded from a 9 to a 5 to a 3 — but it didn't leave. It showed up at unexpected moments. In art class, working on a drawing he liked, the head string would suddenly say: *remember the 58* and the drawing would feel pointless. At recess, laughing with Dex, a flash: *you're the kid who got a 58* and the laughter dimmed.

Shame was a string that contaminated other strings. It leaked into everything, like a drop of ink in water. It turned good moments slightly gray.

On the second day, Kavi noticed something else: the shame wasn't just about the quiz. The 58 had opened a door — not the boredom-door, a different one — and behind it were all the other times he'd gotten something wrong. Second grade, when he misspelled "because" in front of the whole class. Third grade, when he tripped during a relay race and his team lost. Last month, when he gave the wrong answer in math and Rohan snorted.

Every wrong answer was filed in there. Not as events, but as evidence. A growing case file that proved the same thing: *you're not good enough.*

The shame string wasn't pulling him toward anything. It was pulling him away from trying. If you don't try, you can't fail. If you don't fail, the stone in the stomach stays asleep.

---

"What's wrong with getting things wrong?" Thatha said.

It was day three. Porch. Tea. The usual medicine.

"Everything," Kavi said. "When I get something wrong, it feels like I AM wrong."

"And when you get something right?"

"It feels good for like ten seconds and then I forget about it."

"So the wrongs stick and the rights slide off."

"Yeah. Like Velcro and Teflon."

Thatha actually laughed at that. "Where did you learn those words?"

"Ms. Leona. She was explaining surfaces." Kavi paused. "I got that question right, actually."

"And you forgot about it."

"...Yeah."

Thatha set down his tea. The serious-tea-putting-down gesture.

"When I was a young man — maybe twenty — I applied for a job at a university. I was very qualified. I was very prepared. And they rejected me."

"What happened?"

"I stopped applying. For two years. I worked at a job I didn't like because the shame of being rejected was worse than the boredom of the wrong job."

"Two YEARS?"

"Two years. And do you know what I was afraid of?"

"Being rejected again?"

"Being rejected again and it meaning that the first rejection was right. That I wasn't good enough. If I applied and got accepted, the first rejection was a mistake — bad luck, wrong timing. But if I applied and got rejected again, it became a pattern. And a pattern meant it was me."

"That's exactly how I feel about the quiz."

"I know. That's why I'm telling you."

A car passed. Mr. Patel, again, driving slowly enough that Thatha could wave.

"The thing I didn't understand then," Thatha said, "that I understand now — is that the rejection and I were two different things. The rejection happened to me. It wasn't me. The same way rain happens to a tree. The tree gets wet, but the tree is not the rain."

"But the getting wet is real."

"Absolutely. The feeling is real. The sting is real. But the story — 'I am the rejection, I am the 58, I am the failure' — that story is the string. And you don't have to follow it."

"I don't know how to NOT follow it. It's so loud."

"Is it loud right now?"

Kavi checked. The shame was there — a 2, maybe — but it was more like a bruise you're aware of than a wound that's bleeding.

"It's quieter now. Talking about it made it quieter."

"Why?"

"Because... I said it out loud. And the world didn't end. You didn't think I was stupid."

"I don't think you're stupid."

"I know. But the string didn't know that until I tested it."

---

The next day, something happened in math.

Ms. Leona asked a question about fractions — one of the harder ones, the kind with mixed numbers. Kavi knew the answer. He was pretty sure he knew the answer. Maybe 80% sure.

The old Kavi — the one from before the porch conversation — would not have raised his hand. The risk calculation was clear: 20% chance of being wrong, which meant 20% chance of the shame-stone dropping into his stomach, which meant don't try.

But Kavi was tired of the stone. He was tired of carrying it. He was tired of every wrong answer joining the case file, and he was starting to wonder: what if the case file was wrong?

He raised his hand.

Ms. Leona called on him. The spotlight turned on — but he'd practiced with the spotlight, and it was a 4, not an 8.

"Three and one-fourth?" Kavi said.

Silence. The kind that lasts one second but feels like a year.

"That's... not quite," Ms. Leona said. "But you're close. The numerator is right. Who can help Kavi with the denominator?"

The stone dropped. Not as heavy as the 58 stone — more like a pebble. But it dropped. Face warm. Stomach cold. The dual shame-pull.

And then something happened that Kavi didn't expect.

It passed.

Not slowly over three days. Not after a porch conversation. It passed in about thirty seconds. He'd been wrong, in front of everyone, and the world had continued. Dex was doodling. Jacob was picking at his eraser. Ms. Leona had moved on. The wrongness had happened and then it was behind him, and his body — after a thirty-second shame spike — had returned to normal.

*Huh,* Kavi thought. *That's it?*

The head string tried: *Everyone noticed. Everyone thinks you're —*

*No,* Kavi thought back. *That's the spotlight. Nobody cares. They're all under their own lights.*

It was the first time Kavi had talked back to a string and meant it. Not argued with it, not fought it — just corrected it, calmly, the way you'd correct someone who said it was Tuesday when it was actually Wednesday.

*No. That's not what happened.*

The string went quiet. Not silent — quiet. Like it had been heard and was willing to stand down.

---

That evening, Kavi didn't go to the porch. He went to his desk and opened his sketchbook.

He drew the case file.

A filing cabinet — old, metal, dented — with drawers labeled by year. Second grade. Third grade. Fourth grade. Each drawer stuffed with papers, and on each paper, a wrong answer. A misspelled word. A lost race. A bad grade. A moment where he wasn't enough.

Then he drew himself standing in front of the cabinet. Looking at it. And the gingerbread boy's face wasn't ashamed. It was puzzled. Curious. Like he was seeing the cabinet for the first time and wondering who built it.

Because someone had built it. Someone had decided that every wrong answer deserved to be filed. Someone had decided that the evidence mattered. Someone had been building a case — not against the world, but against himself.

And that someone was the shame-string. Working quietly, diligently, for years. Filing every wrong answer. Building the case. Waiting for the verdict.

Under the picture, Kavi wrote: *The shame string keeps a file of everything I've gotten wrong. But it never files the things I've gotten right. That's not evidence. That's a story.*

Then, below that: *Getting something wrong doesn't make me wrong. It makes me someone who tried.*

He almost crossed it out — it sounded like something on a poster in a school counselor's office. But he left it. Because it was true. And because some things that sound simple are the hardest things to actually believe.

---

On Friday, they got their quizzes back. There had been a retake — Ms. Leona offered it to anyone who wanted to improve their score. Kavi had studied this time. Not out of shame. Out of the same curiosity that made him track his strings: he genuinely wanted to know if he could learn the rocks.

He got an 83.

It felt good for about ten seconds. Then he moved on.

But this time, he noticed the moving on. He noticed the good feeling arriving, doing its little dance, and then fading away, like a visitor who only stays for tea. And he noticed that he let it go without trying to hold on — without adding it to a "good" file that needed to be as heavy as the shame file to balance things out.

Maybe the point wasn't to keep files at all.

## Still Thinking?

- Kavi says the first thought after seeing his grade wasn't "I got a bad grade" — it was "I'm stupid." Have you ever had a moment where a bad result became a statement about who you ARE, not just what happened?

- Dex keeps the grade separate from the person: "I'm bad at remembering rock types — that doesn't make me a bad person." Can you do that? Is it easy or hard?

- Kavi discovers a "case file" — a mental collection of every wrong answer, every mistake, every failure. Do you have one? What's in it? Who's been building it?

- "Getting something wrong doesn't make me wrong. It makes me someone who tried." Does that feel true to you, or does it feel like something on a school poster? What's the difference between knowing it and believing it?

- Kavi raised his hand, got it wrong, and the shame passed in thirty seconds. He says "that's it?" Have you ever been afraid of something that turned out to be smaller than you expected? What made the fear bigger than the actual thing?
