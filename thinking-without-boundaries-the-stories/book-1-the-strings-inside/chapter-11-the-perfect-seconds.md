# Chapter 11: The Perfect Seconds

---

It happened on a nothing afternoon in late November.

Kavi was walking home from school because he'd missed the bus — Dex had wanted to show him a caterpillar on the fence by the parking lot, and showing Kavi a caterpillar was apparently more important than catching a bus, and in Dex's defense, it was a really spectacular caterpillar.

So Kavi walked. It was a twelve-minute walk that he usually hated because it cut through the part of the neighborhood where the sidewalk ended and you had to walk on the gravel shoulder, and cars went too fast, and there was nothing to look at.

Except today, there was.

The light was doing something. Kavi didn't know enough about weather to know what — something about the sun being low and the clouds being thin — but the light coming through the trees along Maple Street was gold. Not metaphorical gold. Actual, visible, physical gold, the kind that made the air look like it was full of tiny particles catching fire.

Kavi stopped walking.

The strings... didn't go quiet. They went away.

Not dimmed. Not muffled. Not sleeping. Gone. Like someone had opened a window and they'd blown out, all at once, leaving behind a room so empty and so clean that Kavi could hear his own heartbeat, and even that sounded beautiful.

The light came through the trees and landed on the gravel at his feet and it was the most extraordinary thing he'd ever seen. Not because it was special. Because it was ordinary. This happened every day — light through trees, gold on gravel — and nobody stopped, nobody looked, nobody felt their whole body go still from the sheer fact of it.

Kavi stood on the gravel shoulder of Maple Street with his backpack on and his mouth slightly open and felt something he had no word for.

It wasn't happiness. Happiness was something you felt because of a reason — a good grade, a funny joke, a pizza Friday. This was reason-less. It was just there, rising from everywhere at once — the light, the air, the sound of birds, the crunch of gravel, his own breathing — like the world itself was the reason and the feeling was the response and there was nothing between them. No string. No filter. No story.

Just this.

It lasted maybe seven seconds. Then a car drove past, loud and fast, and the light shifted as a cloud moved, and Kavi's chest string woke up (*better keep walking, you look weird standing here*) and the moment folded up and slipped away like a letter into an envelope.

Kavi stood there for another few seconds, breathing, trying to hold onto it. But the trying was already the losing. The moment wasn't something you could hold. It was something that held you, briefly, and then let go.

He kept walking. The gravel crunched. The light was still gold, but now it was just light. Beautiful, but not the same. The window had closed.

But he'd been inside. For seven seconds, he'd been inside something that felt like the truest thing he'd ever experienced.

---

He didn't tell Thatha right away.

Not because it was too big — although it was — but because he was afraid that talking about it would change it. That words would flatten it into something smaller than it was. Like pressing a flower in a book — you keep the shape, but you lose the aliveness.

Instead, he went to his room and sat on his bed and replayed the seven seconds, over and over, trying to find the edges of what had happened.

The strings had been gone. That was the first thing. Not fought, not watched, not released through contraction or looking-sideways or any of the tricks he'd been learning. Just gone. As if they'd never existed. As if the machinery that produced them — the worrying, the predicting, the comparing, the protecting — had simply paused.

And in the pause: the world, in full color, without narration.

The second thing: he hadn't been Kavi. Not the Appa-Kavi or the Dex-Kavi or any of the shapes. Not even the water underneath the shapes. He'd just been... looking. No looker. Just looking. Like the seeing was happening but nobody was doing it.

This was hard to hold in his brain. How can seeing happen without a seer? That was like asking how rain could fall without a cloud. But that was what it had felt like — rain without a cloud. Pure happening. Pure is-ness.

*This is weird,* the head string said, now fully recovered from its seven-second vacation. *This is really, really weird and you shouldn't think about it too hard or you'll mess it up.*

For once, the head string was right. Kavi stopped trying to analyze it and just sat with the memory like a warm stone in his pocket.

---

Over the next two weeks, the perfect seconds came and went.

They never lasted long — the longest was maybe twelve seconds, during a rainstorm when he watched water run down the kitchen window and the pattern of the rivulets became so absorbing that everything else dissolved.

They never came when he tried. He tried once, standing on Maple Street at the same time of day, waiting for the gold light. The light came. The moment didn't. His trying was a string, and strings, by definition, were the opposite of whatever the perfect seconds were.

They always came uninvited. In the middle of ordinary things:

**Washing dishes.** Amma had asked him to wash his own plate after dinner. He was standing at the sink, warm water running over his hands, soap making small rainbows, and for four seconds, the water was the only thing in the universe.

**Sitting in class.** Ms. Leona was reading out loud from a book about a boy who found a bird's nest. Her voice had a rhythm — low, even, warm — and the words stopped being words and became sounds, and the sounds became a pattern, and the pattern opened a space, and in the space: three seconds of no-Kavi.

**Drawing.** This one was different because it lasted longer — maybe thirty seconds — and it came from inside the doing rather than from watching. He was drawing a tree, and his hand was moving, and the pencil was making marks, and somewhere in the middle of it, the drawer and the drawing merged and there was just the act, just the line appearing on paper, just the gray graphite becoming something.

Each time, the strings came back. Each time, the world reassembled around him — the noise, the thoughts, the body, the Kavi-ness. But each time, there was a faint after-shimmer, like the image left on your eyes after looking at something bright.

---

"Have you ever felt it?" Kavi asked Thatha. It was a Saturday. They were repotting a plant on the porch.

"Felt what?"

"Like... a moment where everything goes quiet — really quiet — and the world is just there and you're just there and there's nothing between you and it. No thoughts. No strings. Nothing."

Thatha's hands were in the soil. He paused. Not the thinking-pause. The remembering-pause. His face did something Kavi had never seen — it softened in a way that made him look not younger but outside of age altogether, like the concept of years had briefly stopped applying.

"Yes," he said. "I've felt it."

"What is it?"

"I don't know."

Kavi was surprised. Thatha usually had at least a question to offer in place of an answer. But this was different. Thatha genuinely didn't know, and he was saying so without trying to dress it up.

"The first time it happened to me," Thatha continued, "I was about sixteen. I was sitting by a river — a real river, in the village where I grew up. And the water was moving, and the sun was on it, and something... opened. For maybe twenty seconds. No me. No river. Just the opening."

"That's exactly it. No me."

"And then it closed, and I spent the next forty years trying to get it back."

"Did you?"

Thatha laughed softly. "It doesn't work like that. The trying is the thing that prevents it. I went to temples. I meditated. I read books by people who said they knew the secret. And none of it brought back those twenty seconds."

"So what did?"

"Stopping. The same way you found the quiet between the drips — not by looking for it, but by stopping the looking."

"But you can't choose to stop."

"No. But you can stop choosing. There's a difference."

Kavi put a plant in the new pot. Soil in his fingernails. The sun was warm. A bee visited the flowers and left.

"Thatha? Is the moment... the real thing? Like, is that what life actually is, underneath everything?"

Thatha pressed soil around the base of the plant, gentle, firm.

"I don't know that either. But I know that every time it happens, I feel like I've come home. And every time it ends, I feel like I've left a place I didn't know I was missing."

"Home."

"Home. Not a place. A way of being there."

---

That night, Kavi opened his sketchbook to a blank page and didn't draw.

He stared at the white paper. It was already complete. The blankness wasn't empty — it was full. Full of everything that could be drawn on it, everything that hadn't been decided yet. The paper was possibility, unfiltered, untouched.

He thought about the perfect seconds. How they came without strings. How they couldn't be drawn or named or held. How the trying killed them and the not-trying invited them.

He thought about all the strings he'd named — the chest hum, the stomach clench, the head chatter, the anger, the shame, the spotlight, the labels, the scroll. He'd spent weeks watching them, tracking them, drawing them, understanding them. And that was good. That was the first thing, like Thatha said.

But the perfect seconds were something else. They weren't about the strings at all. They were about what was already there when the strings weren't pulling. Not something he'd built or earned or achieved. Something that had always been there, underneath, like the white paper under every drawing.

He picked up his pencil and wrote one sentence in the middle of the blank page:

*I don't need to add anything. I just need to stop covering it up.*

Then he closed the sketchbook, turned off the light, and lay in the dark.

The strings were there. The hum, the low-level chatter, the usual cast of characters. But behind them — not far, not hidden, just behind — the blank page. The gold light. The seven seconds.

Waiting. Not going anywhere. Just waiting.

## Still Thinking?

- Kavi's "perfect seconds" happen during ordinary moments — light through trees, water on hands, a voice reading out loud. Have you ever had a moment like that? Where something ordinary suddenly felt extraordinary — not because it changed, but because you did?

- The perfect seconds never come when Kavi tries to make them happen. Why do you think that is? What is "trying" actually doing that blocks the moment?

- Thatha says he spent forty years trying to get back to a twenty-second experience by a river. Then he found it again by stopping the search. Does "you can't choose to stop, but you can stop choosing" make any sense to you? What's the difference?

- Kavi notices that the perfect seconds are "what's already there when the strings aren't pulling." Think about the last time you felt completely at peace. Were you doing something or nothing? Were you trying, or had you stopped?

- At the end, Kavi writes: "I don't need to add anything. I just need to stop covering it up." What do you think he means? What are the things that cover it up?
