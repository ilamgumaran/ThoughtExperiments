# Characters

## Main Characters

### Kavi Rajan — Age 9, 4th Grade
**The Noticer**

Kavi is the kind of kid who stares at things too long. He watches ants carry crumbs across sidewalks. He notices when his teacher's smile doesn't match her voice. He's not trying to be deep — he's just wired to pay attention to things other kids skip over.

**What makes him tick:**
- Curious but not academic — he doesn't love school, but he loves understanding
- Gets overwhelmed easily — loud cafeterias, sudden changes, too many instructions at once
- Has a temper that surprises him — it flares up fast, and afterward he doesn't understand why
- Loves drawing — it's the one place where his brain goes quiet
- Asks "why" about things adults have stopped questioning ("Why do we have to sit still to learn?")

**His arc in Book 1:** Kavi discovers that the feelings that overwhelm him aren't random — they follow patterns. He starts calling them "strings" and learns that noticing a string isn't the same as following it. This doesn't make life easier, but it makes it less confusing.

**What he struggles with:**
- He thinks something is wrong with him because he feels things so strongly
- He compares himself to kids who seem to move through life without friction
- He wants to be "normal" but can't figure out what that means

---

### Meera Rajan — Age 12, 7th Grade
**The Questioner**

Meera is Kavi's older sister. She's smart, socially aware, and increasingly frustrated by the gap between how things are and how they should be. She's at the age where she's starting to see through adult logic but doesn't have her own logic yet to replace it.

**What makes her tick:**
- Sharp observer of social dynamics — she sees the invisible hierarchies in every room
- Caught between wanting to fit in and wanting to be honest
- Reads a lot — but is starting to notice that books give cleaner answers than real life does
- Protective of Kavi, sometimes too much — she feels responsible for him
- Beginning to ask identity questions: "Am I this way because I chose it, or because everyone expects it?"

**Her arc in Book 2:** Meera starts mapping the unwritten rules of her social world and realizes most of them are inherited, not chosen. She experiments with what happens when she stops following rules she never agreed to.

**What she struggles with:**
- The pressure to have opinions about everything and perform certainty
- Feeling like she's "too much" for some friends and "not enough" for others
- The gap between who she is at home vs. school vs. online

## Supporting Characters

### Thatha (Grandfather) — Age 68
**The One Who Listens**

Thatha lives with the family. He's retired, quiet, and spends most of his time in the garden or on the porch. He doesn't give advice unless asked, and even then, he usually answers with a question.

He's not a guru figure — he's a man who has lived long enough to stop pretending he has answers. His role is to be present when Kavi or Meera need someone who won't try to fix them.

**Key trait:** He never says "you should." He says "what do you think?" or "what did you notice?"

**His relationship with Kavi:** They sit together on the porch sometimes. Thatha taught Kavi to watch ants. He's the one person Kavi doesn't feel "too much" around.

**His relationship with Meera:** More complicated. Meera sometimes finds him frustrating because he won't take sides. But she also knows he's the only adult who doesn't talk down to her.

---

### Amma (Mother) — Age 40
Practical, loving, busy. Works as a nurse. She doesn't always have time for long conversations but shows care through action — packed lunches with notes, showing up to every school event despite exhaustion. She worries about both kids but handles it differently: she hovers over Kavi and gives Meera too much independence too early.

### Appa (Father) — Age 42
An engineer who believes in systems and solutions. He loves his kids deeply but expresses it through problem-solving, which doesn't always land. When Kavi is upset, Appa wants to fix it. When Meera is angry, Appa wants to explain why she shouldn't be. He's learning, slowly, that presence matters more than answers.

---

### Kavi's World

**Dex** — Age 9. Kavi's best friend. Loud, funny, moves through life like nothing sticks. Kavi both admires and envies him. Dex seems immune to strings — but later we see he just hides them differently.

**Ms. Leona** — 4th grade teacher. Kind but overworked. She notices Kavi's sensitivity and sometimes gives him space, sometimes doesn't know how. She represents the adult who means well but operates within a system that doesn't flex.

**Rohan** — Age 10. The kid who says the things others think but don't say. Not a bully exactly, but blunt in a way that cuts. He becomes an important mirror for Kavi — showing that some "strings" come from outside, not inside.

---

### Meera's World

**Suki** — Age 12. Meera's closest friend since 3rd grade. They're drifting apart because Suki is moving toward the "popular" group and Meera can't bring herself to play the game. Their friendship becomes a central tension in Book 2.

**Jas** — Age 13. New student. Quiet, artistic, doesn't fit any category. Meera is drawn to Jas because Jas seems genuinely unbothered by social rules — not in a defiant way, but in a way that suggests they never agreed to them in the first place.

**Mr. Padilla** — 7th grade social studies teacher. The teacher who asks questions he doesn't have answers to. He creates space for real thinking in class, which both excites and unnerves Meera.

## Character Design Principles

1. **No villains.** Every character has their own strings pulling them. Even the kids who say hurtful things are dealing with their own interference.

2. **Adults are imperfect.** Parents and teachers are trying their best within their own limitations. They're not obstacles — they're people still figuring it out.

3. **Growth is nonlinear.** Characters have breakthroughs and then forget them. They understand something on Tuesday and lose it by Friday. This is honest.

4. **Diversity is ambient, not performative.** The cast reflects the real world — different backgrounds, family structures, neurotypes — without making any character a representative of their group.

5. **Internal life matters more than plot.** What a character notices, feels, and questions matters more than what happens to them.
