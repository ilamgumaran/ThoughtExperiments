# Chapter 11: Still Water

---

Winter break came, and with it, silence.

Not the school-silence of indoor recess or library time. Real silence. The kind that belongs to houses at 6 AM when nobody else is awake and the world hasn't started yet.

Nila discovered it by accident. She'd been waking up early all semester — the antenna humming with school signals, the fire smoldering from yesterday's injustice, the body alert and watchful. Her sleep was restless and her mornings were electric.

But on the first morning of winter break, she woke at 6 AM and the antenna was quiet.

Not broken. Not off. Just... resting. Like it had been working double shifts for four months and had finally been given a day off.

She lay in bed and listened to the house. Appa's alarm wouldn't go off for an hour. Amma was working night shift and wouldn't be home until 7. Adi was buried in blankets, the small mountain of a sleeping seven-year-old. And Paati — Paati was already awake, because Paati was always already awake, but she was somewhere in the house being silent in the way only Paati could.

Nila listened to the nothing and felt her body do something it hadn't done in months: relax. Not the forced relaxation of "take a deep breath" or "count to ten." The real kind. The kind where every muscle simultaneously remembers that tension is optional and agrees to let go.

Her shoulders dropped. Her jaw unclenched. Her hands, which she'd apparently been keeping in loose fists while sleeping, opened flat on the mattress.

And in the space that the tension left behind — in the gap where the fire usually lived and the antenna usually hummed — there was something she didn't recognize.

Not empty. Not quiet, exactly. More like... still.

The way a pond is still at dawn. Not dead — alive, completely alive, bugs skating on the surface, fish moving below — but still. Undisturbed from outside. Settled from within.

---

Nila lay there for eleven minutes. She knew because she checked the clock before and after, and the clock said 6:03 and then 6:14, which meant that the longest period of peace she'd experienced since September had lasted roughly as long as an episode of the cartoon Adi watched before dinner.

But in those eleven minutes, something happened. Or rather, something stopped happening. The voice in her head — the constant narrator that said *what's next, who needs me, what's wrong, what should I fix* — went quiet. Not forever. Just for a visit. A vacation from being Nila.

And without the narrator, without the fire, without the antenna scanning for signals, Nila was just... a person in a bed. Breathing. Warm. Here.

She tried to hold onto it. The moment she tried, the narrator came back: *this is nice, I should try to keep this, why can't I feel this way all the time, maybe if I wake up early every day—*

And just like that, the still water rippled.

---

"Paati, I found something."

6:30 AM. Kitchen. Paati was making filter coffee, the pour-back-and-forth technique that was half cooking and half ceremony.

"This early, you find something?"

"I woke up and everything was quiet. Not just the house — inside me. The fire was out. The antenna was off. And there was this... stillness. Like a pond."

Paati poured the coffee between the cups. Long arc. Perfect stream. "You found it."

"Found what? I didn't even know I was looking."

"That's why you found it." Paati set one cup down. "Looking is noise. Finding happens when looking stops."

"But how do I get it back? I tried to hold onto it and it went away."

Paati laughed. The small one that meant Nila had asked a question Paati had been waiting for.

"Do you hold onto sleep?"

"What?"

"When you fall asleep — do you grab it? Do you hold it?"

"No. It just... happens."

"And when you try too hard to sleep?"

"I can't sleep. Trying to sleep is the thing that keeps me awake."

"Still water is same. You don't grab it. You don't make it. You stop the things that make waves. And the water settles by itself."

"But I can't control when the waves stop."

"No. But you notice when they have stopped. And in the noticing — if you notice gently, like looking at a butterfly, not grabbing — the stillness stays a little longer."

---

Nila spent winter break learning the difference between stillness and trying-to-be-still.

It turned out they were opposites.

Trying-to-be-still was another wave. A particularly sneaky wave, because it dressed up as calm but was actually effort — the effort of monitoring herself, checking her fire levels, adjusting her antenna, managing her breath. "Am I still yet? Am I still yet? How about now?" Each question was a pebble dropped in the pond.

Actual stillness came uninvited.

It came while washing dishes — the warm water, the repetitive motion, the soap bubbles catching light. Five seconds of no-narrator.

It came while walking the dog they didn't have (she was walking Adi, who moved at dog speed and required similar supervision). Adi was splashing in a puddle and Nila was watching, and the watching became so complete that the watcher dissolved, and for three seconds there was just a boy and a puddle and light.

It came while sitting with Paati on the living room floor, sorting lentils. The same ancient, meditative task as the rice — picking out stones, moving the good ones, a rhythm older than memory. Nila's hands moved automatically, and her mind — with nothing to fight, nothing to fix, nobody to protect — settled. Six seconds. Maybe seven.

Each time, the stillness was underneath. Not something she achieved. Something she fell into when the achieving stopped.

Each time, the fire was somewhere else. Not gone — she could find it if she looked — but not driving. Parked. Engine idling. Ready if needed, but not insisting.

---

On the second-to-last day of break, Nila had the longest period of stillness yet.

She was in her room. Rain outside — the good kind, steady and grey, the kind that made the world smaller and softer. She was sitting on her bed doing nothing, which was unusual for Nila, who was usually doing seven things or thinking about doing seven things or feeling guilty about not doing seven things.

But today, she sat. And the rain fell. And the stillness came.

Not suddenly. Gradually. Like the water in a shaken snow globe slowly settling, the tiny flakes drifting downward, the scene inside becoming clear.

Her body was warm. Her breath was even. Her antenna was on — she could feel Adi's energy downstairs, Paati's calm in the next room, the rain's rhythm on the roof — but instead of absorbing every signal and adding it to her load, the signals were passing through. Coming in, moving through, going out. The colander. The coffee filter. The rain through a tree.

And what was left — what settled at the bottom when the snow globe stilled — was something Nila had never felt before.

Not happiness. Not peace. Not the absence of fire. Something more fundamental. The thing that was there before the fire started and would be there after it went out. The thing that was there before the antenna turned on and would be there if the antenna broke. The thing underneath all the feelings, underneath the caring and the rage and the sadness and the joy.

She didn't have a word for it. It wasn't a feeling. It was more like... the surface on which feelings happened. The pond itself, not the ripples. The sky itself, not the clouds.

She was the water. She'd always been the water. The fire, the waves, the signals, the caring — they were what happened ON the water. But the water itself was still. Had always been still.

Even when the waves were crashing.

Even when the fire was raging.

Even when the antenna was screaming.

The water was still.

---

It lasted maybe forty seconds. Then Adi yelled something downstairs and her body jumped and the snow globe shook and the flakes went everywhere and she was Nila again — caring, burning, feeling, receiving, everything at full volume.

But those forty seconds stayed with her. Not as a memory — as an imprint. Like the feeling of the ground under your feet. You don't think about it, but it's there. Holding you up. Always holding you up.

---

"Paati. I found it again. But longer."

Last day of break. Paati was packing lunchboxes for school tomorrow, returning to routine with the quiet efficiency of someone who had never been off routine.

"How long?"

"Forty seconds. Maybe."

Paati smiled. "Forty seconds is a lot."

"It doesn't seem like a lot."

"One match is small. But one match lights a lamp. One lamp lights a room."

"So forty seconds of stillness is my match?"

"Your match. And it will burn as long as you don't blow it out by grabbing."

"Is this what you feel? When you sort rice? When you make coffee?"

Paati considered this. "I feel it more often now. Because I am old, and old people have less waves. But when I was young — your age, your mother's age — the waves were big. The fire was big. I was big antenna too."

"You?"

"Me. Where do you think you got the antenna?"

Nila had never considered this. Paati — quiet, steady, fortune-cookie Paati — had been like Nila? Had felt everything, burned with justice, absorbed the room?

"The waves don't stop," Paati said. "You go back to school tomorrow. Marcus will be there. Ethan will be there. Children will tease and cry and laugh and hurt. Your antenna will pick up everything. Your fire will want to burn."

"So the stillness was just a vacation?"

"No. The stillness is always there. Under the waves. Like floor under water. You felt the floor. You know it's there now. Even when the water is high, the floor doesn't move."

"So I can find it at school?"

"You can know it's there at school. Finding it... that's harder. But knowing it's there changes how you stand in the waves."

---

Nila went to bed the night before school started and felt the waves already building. Tomorrow was coming with all its signals and fires and stages and people. The antenna was warming up. The narrator was drafting scenarios.

But underneath — far underneath, in the place where the forty seconds lived — the still water was there.

She didn't try to reach it. She didn't try to hold it. She just knew it was there. The way you know the ground is there without looking down.

She fell asleep standing on it.

## Still Thinking?

- Nila's stillness came "when looking stopped." Have you ever found something — an idea, a feeling, a state of calm — that you couldn't find when you were looking for it?

- "Trying-to-be-still is another wave." Does that match your experience? When adults tell you to "calm down," does the trying help or make it harder?

- Nila felt something underneath all her feelings — "the surface on which feelings happened." Like a pond underneath its own waves. Does that image make sense to you? Is there a "you" underneath all your emotions?

- Paati says the stillness is "like floor under water — even when the water is high, the floor doesn't move." If the floor is always there, why is it so hard to feel?

- School starts again tomorrow. Nila's forty seconds of stillness will be tested by real situations. Do you think knowing the still water is there changes anything? Or does it only matter if you can actually feel it?
