# Chapter 2: The Laughter That Isn't Funny

---

The next morning, Nila decided to watch.

Not react. Not fix. Not burn. Just watch. The way a scientist watches an experiment — taking notes, staying behind the glass, letting the thing happen without touching it.

This lasted approximately forty-five minutes.

---

She made it through the bus ride (no incidents — the bus was mostly sleepy kids and phone screens). She made it through the hallway (her antenna was picking up signals, but she let them pass through instead of catching them, like holding a net loosely instead of tight). She made it all the way to Mrs. Callahan's class and was feeling genuinely good about her new plan.

Then Marcus pulled a chair out from under Lily Chen as she was sitting down.

It was quick. Practiced. Marcus's hand shot out, the chair slid back three inches — just enough — and Lily sat on air for a half second before catching herself on the desk. She didn't fall. But her face did the thing — the flash of terror, then embarrassment, then anger, all in the space of a heartbeat.

The class laughed.

Not everyone. Not Priya (who looked pained). Not Jaden (who stared at his desk). Not Mrs. Callahan (who was writing on the board and didn't see). But enough kids laughed that the room filled with it — a sharp, electric sound that bounced off the walls and landed on Lily's burning face.

Nila's chest ignited.

*So much for watching.*

But here's the thing she noticed, in the half second between the fire lighting and the fire speaking: the laughter wasn't one kind of laughter.

She heard it. Really heard it, the way you hear a chord instead of just a note. And the chord had layers:

There was surprise-laughter — the involuntary kind, the hiccup of seeing something unexpected. This was the loudest layer. Most kids were laughing because their brains processed "unexpected thing" and produced "laugh" before they could choose not to.

There was nervous-laughter — the kind that comes from relief. *That wasn't me. I'm glad that wasn't me.* This was the second loudest.

There was mean-laughter — the kind that's directed AT someone, that wants the person to hear it, that feeds on the embarrassment. This was actually the quietest layer. Only two or three kids were laughing this way.

And there was Marcus's laugh. Which was something else entirely.

Marcus's laugh was performance. It was loud and it was watching Lily's face while it happened, and it was checking the room — scanning left to right, measuring the response, counting the audience. His laugh wasn't about the chair. It was about the reaction to the chair. He was watching his joke land the way a musician watches the crowd during a solo.

All of this happened in about two seconds. Then Nila's fire took over and she was out of the observation seat and into the arena.

"That's NOT funny, Marcus."

Marcus turned to her. The grin again. "It's a little funny."

"She could have gotten hurt."

"She didn't get hurt. She's fine. Right, Lily?"

Lily said, "I'm fine," in a voice that was clearly not fine, and Nila's antenna screamed because she could feel Lily's humiliation as clearly as if it were her own — the hot face, the wish to disappear, the awful performance of "fine" for an audience that was still deciding whether to keep laughing.

"Nila, that's enough," Mrs. Callahan said, finally turned around. "Marcus, keep your hands to yourself. Lily, are you okay?"

"I'm fine," Lily said again. The word "fine" was doing so much heavy lifting that Nila was surprised it didn't collapse.

Mrs. Callahan moved on. The class moved on. Marcus moved on. Lily stared at her desk for three straight minutes.

Nila's fire burned for the rest of the morning.

---

At recess, Nila found Priya on their usual bench near the garden and asked the question that had been building since yesterday.

"Why do people laugh when someone gets hurt?"

Priya considered this seriously, because Priya considered everything seriously. "Which kind of hurt? Physical hurt or feeling hurt?"

"Both. Either. Lily could have fallen. And even though she didn't, she was embarrassed. And people LAUGHED."

"I think the laughing happens before the thinking. Like, the chair thing is surprising, and surprises make people laugh. It's not that they think it's funny that Lily's embarrassed."

"Some of them did."

"Some of them. But most of them just laughed because their brains said 'unexpected.'"

"But that's still wrong. You shouldn't laugh when someone could get hurt."

"I know. But 'should' and 'do' are different things."

Nila pulled at a loose thread on her jacket. The fire was down to a 5 now — present but not raging. Priya had that effect on her. Being near Priya was like sitting near a cool river when you were overheated.

"Can I tell you something weird?" Nila said.

"Always."

"Right before I yelled at Marcus, I heard the laughter. Really heard it. And it wasn't all the same. Some kids were surprised. Some were nervous. Some were actually mean. And Marcus — Marcus wasn't laughing at Lily at all. He was laughing at... the effect. The reaction. He was watching the room like he'd pressed a button and wanted to see what happened."

"Like an experiment."

"Yeah. Like we're his experiment."

Priya was quiet for a moment. A bee visited the garden flowers. A basketball bounced somewhere distant.

"So if he's running an experiment," Priya said slowly, "what's he testing?"

"How people react. Whether the joke works. Whether people laugh."

"And when you yelled at him?"

The question landed in Nila's stomach like a cold coin.

"He smiled. He grinned. Like I was... the best part."

"Maybe to him, you were."

Nila put her face in her hands. "So my anger is his punchline?"

"I don't think it's that simple. But I think... your reaction is part of what makes it a show. Without an audience, there's no show."

---

The idea followed Nila home like a dog she hadn't asked to adopt.

*Without an audience, there's no show.*

She sat at the kitchen table doing homework — fractions, which she could do in her sleep — but her brain was elsewhere. It was replaying every time she'd confronted Marcus. Every time she'd stood up, voice shaking, face hot, and told him to stop. Every time he'd grinned.

The grin. She'd always read it as defiance. *I don't care what you think.* But what if it wasn't defiance? What if it was satisfaction? What if every time Nila's fire blazed, Marcus felt something he didn't get anywhere else — the full attention of someone who cared deeply, pointed directly at him?

Not love-attention. Not friend-attention. But attention. Raw, intense, undeniable. The kind of attention that says *you exist, you matter, what you did had an effect.*

"Paati?" Nila said.

Paati was sorting rice at the counter. Picking out stones. An ancient, meditative task she did every evening.

"When Adi does something naughty to get attention... what do you do?"

Paati picked up a stone. Small, gray, hiding among the rice. Set it aside.

"Nothing," Paati said.

"Nothing?"

"If Adi throws toy — I don't run. I sit. He sees: throwing didn't work. He stops."

"But what if he doesn't stop?"

"He stops. Maybe not first time. Maybe not second. But if throwing never works, throwing is not interesting."

"But what about the thing he threw? What if he broke something?"

Paati smiled. "Then we talk about the broken thing. But calmly. Not running. Running is the game."

*Running is the game.*

Nila's fire spiked at this — because not running felt wrong. Not running felt like letting it happen. Not running felt like watching Lily almost fall and saying nothing.

"Paati, but what if someone is HURT? Not like Adi throwing a toy. Like someone getting teased. Someone being embarrassed. You can't just do nothing."

Paati set another stone aside. Her fingers were precise, unhurried.

"Nothing and calm are not same," she said.

Nila's pencil stopped moving over her fractions.

"Nothing means you don't care. Calm means you care but the fire doesn't drive."

Paati tapped her own chest. Then her head. Then she made a driving motion with her hands.

"Fire here" — chest. "Thinking here" — head. "Which one drives?"

"The fire drives. For me, the fire always drives."

"Mmm." Paati went back to her rice. Stone. Aside. Stone. Aside. "Fire is good engine. Bad driver."

---

That night, Nila couldn't sleep.

She lay in bed and felt her antenna picking up the house — Adi's breathing, Appa's late-night typing, Amma's restless turning. Paati's silence, which had its own frequency, like a radio station that played nothing but stillness.

The fire was at a 1. Almost out. But the coals were warm, and the question was sitting in them:

*How do you care without burning?*

Because she did care. She cared when Lily almost fell. She cared when Jaden's neck went red. She cared when kids laughed at someone's pain, even the kids who didn't mean to be cruel, even the kids who were just surprised.

She cared, and the caring turned into fire, and the fire turned into reaction, and the reaction turned into Marcus's grin.

She was the fuel. Not intentionally. Not willingly. But every time the fire blazed, it lit the stage, and the show went on.

*Fire is good engine. Bad driver.*

If the fire doesn't drive... what does?

Nila didn't know yet. But the question was there, warm in the coals, and she carried it into sleep the way Paati carried the stones out of the rice — carefully, one at a time, not rushing.

## Still Thinking?

- Nila heard layers in the class's laughter — surprise, nervousness, meanness, and performance. Think about the last time people laughed at something that wasn't entirely funny. How many kinds of laughter were in it?

- Marcus watches the room after his prank "like a musician watching the crowd during a solo." Have you ever noticed someone checking to see if their joke landed? What are they really looking for?

- Priya says: "Without an audience, there's no show." Does that mean the audience is responsible for the show? Or is the performer responsible? Or both?

- Paati says "nothing and calm are not same." What's the difference? Can you do something about a situation without the fire driving?

- "Fire is good engine. Bad driver." Think about a time you felt a strong reaction to something unfair. Did the fire help you, or did it take over? What would it look like if something else was driving?
