# Chapter 1: The Girl Who Felt Everything

---

Nila Sundaram cried at commercials.

Not all commercials. Not the ones about car insurance or cleaning products or vitamins for adults. But the ones with dogs — the ones where a dog waits by the door for its person to come home, and the music swells, and the door finally opens — those got her every time.

"It's a COMMERCIAL, Nila," her brother Adi said from the other end of the couch. Adi was seven and had the emotional range of a calculator.

"It's a dog who MISSED someone," Nila said, wiping her eyes with her sleeve. "That's real. Dogs really do that."

"It's selling you a phone plan."

"I don't care what it's selling. The dog is real."

Adi went back to building something out of couch cushions. Nila went back to feeling too much about a thirty-second advertisement for a telecommunications company.

This was the thing about Nila. She didn't have a volume knob. Other people seemed to have one — they could hear a sad story and turn it down to a 3, process it in the background, move on. Nila's volume was stuck at 10. Every sad thing was the saddest thing. Every happy thing was the happiest thing. Every unfair thing was an injustice that vibrated in her chest like a bell that had been struck too hard.

Her mother called it "being sensitive." Her father called it "having a big heart." Nila called it exhausting.

---

Morning at the Sundaram house was a controlled explosion.

Amma (Nila's mother, not to be confused with the Amma in Kavi's house three neighborhoods over — there were a lot of Ammas) was packing lunches while simultaneously talking on the phone to the store manager about a delivery that was late. Appa was ironing his shirt because he'd forgotten to do it last night. Adi was looking for his left shoe, which he'd left somewhere impossible, as he did every morning.

And Paati — Nila's grandmother — was sitting at the kitchen table with a cup of filter coffee, watching all of this with the serene patience of someone who had spent sixty-seven years learning that mornings are chaos and chaos passes.

Nila sat next to Paati and ate her idli. The sambar was perfect — Paati had made it at 5 AM, because Paati's clock ran on its own schedule — and Nila dunked each piece slowly, savoring the warmth.

"Paati, do you ever feel other people's feelings?"

Paati looked at her. Paati's English was limited, but her understanding was not. She had a way of hearing the question underneath the question.

"Feel?" Paati said, touching her own chest.

"Yeah. Like, when Adi is upset, my stomach hurts. When Amma is stressed, my shoulders get tight. It's like their feelings are contagious. Like I catch them."

Paati considered this. She took a sip of coffee.

"Antenna," she said, tapping Nila's head gently.

"Antenna?"

"You — big antenna. Pick up everything." She made a gesture like a satellite dish, rotating, receiving signals from every direction. "Some people — small antenna. Pick up little bit. You — big."

"I don't want a big antenna."

"Antenna not bad. Just..." Paati searched for the word. "Loud."

That was exactly right. Nila's antenna was loud. It picked up every signal in every room, and it played them all at once, at full volume, directly into her chest.

---

School was the hardest place to have a big antenna.

Ridgemont Elementary had four hundred and twenty kids, which meant four hundred and twenty signals at any given time. Happy signals, nervous signals, bored signals, angry signals — all of them broadcasting, all of them hitting Nila's antenna whether she wanted them or not.

Walking down the hallway was like walking through a rainstorm of feelings. That kid was anxious (she could see it in his hands, the way he kept opening and closing them). That girl was pretending to laugh but her eyes were tight (the laugh was a wall, not a window). That teacher was tired — bone tired, the kind that lived in her posture, in the way she leaned against the doorframe like it was holding her up.

Nila felt all of it. The anxiety made her stomach clench. The fake laugh made her chest ache. The tiredness pulled at her shoulders.

By the time she reached Mrs. Callahan's classroom, she'd already absorbed fifteen feelings that weren't hers.

"You okay?" said Priya, who was already at her desk, arranged and organized because Priya was the kind of person who made order look effortless.

"Yeah. The hallway is a lot."

Priya nodded. Priya understood, in her quiet way, that Nila experienced school differently. Not harder, not worse — just louder.

---

The morning was fine until second period.

Mrs. Callahan was teaching about ecosystems — food chains, producers, consumers, decomposers — and Nila was following along, taking notes in her color-coded system (green for plants, red for predators, brown for decomposers, because organization was one way to manage the chaos inside her). The room was calm. Twenty-eight kids, mostly settled, the usual low hum of bodies in chairs.

Then Marcus walked in late.

Marcus Webb always entered a room like a small weather event. Not loud exactly — more like a shift in pressure. You felt him arrive before you saw him. The energy in the room rearranged. Kids sat up a little. Mrs. Callahan's jaw tightened a little. The air got slightly more electric.

"Nice of you to join us, Marcus," Mrs. Callahan said.

"I was consulting with the principal about important matters," Marcus said, which meant he'd been in the office for something he'd done in first period.

He walked to his desk, which was two rows behind Nila, and sat down. On his way, he flicked the back of Jaden's ear — just a tiny flick, barely anything — and whispered something that made Jaden's neck go red.

Nila's antenna caught it. All of it. The flick. Jaden's reaction — the redness, the way his shoulders came up around his ears like armor. The flash of something in Jaden's eyes that was too fast to name but felt, through Nila's antenna, like being poked in a bruise.

Her chest did the thing.

The thing was this: a heat that started at the center of her ribcage and expanded outward like a dropped stone making rings in water. Not gentle rings. Fast, hard rings that hit the edges of her body within two seconds and made her fingers tingle and her jaw clench and her eyes go sharp.

This was Nila's fire. Her justice fire. The thing that lit up whenever something was unfair and said, in a voice that drowned out everything else: *DO SOMETHING.*

She turned around in her chair.

"Leave him alone, Marcus."

Marcus looked at her with the particular expression of a kid who has been handed exactly what he wanted. His eyes widened — not with surprise, but with something else. Delight. The delight of a fisherman who'd just felt a tug on his line.

"I didn't do anything," he said, grinning.

"You flicked his ear."

"It was a greeting. Jaden and I are friends. Right, Jaden?"

Jaden said nothing. His neck was still red.

"Nila, turn around please," Mrs. Callahan said.

Nila turned around. The fire was still burning. It didn't care about Mrs. Callahan's instruction. It cared about Jaden's red neck and Marcus's grin and the fundamental, screaming wrongness of someone hurting someone else and calling it a greeting.

She gripped her pencil. The green one. Producers. Plants. Things that grow quietly.

The fire didn't go quietly. It sat in her chest for the rest of the period, smoldering, telling her she should have said more, done more, been more.

---

At lunch, Nila sat with Priya and replayed it.

"He FLICKED his ear. And then he smiled. Like it was funny."

"It wasn't funny," Priya agreed.

"And Jaden didn't say anything. He just sat there. Why didn't he say anything?"

"Maybe he didn't want to make it bigger."

"But it IS big. You can't just touch someone and call it a greeting. That's not what greetings are."

Priya unwrapped her sandwich — peanut butter and banana, the same thing every day, because Priya found comfort in routine. "Nila, you're shaking."

Nila looked at her hands. She was shaking. Not a lot. But the tremor was there, visible, the fire still working its way through her body two hours after a two-second incident.

"It just... it makes me so MAD. Why can't people just be nice? It's not hard. Being nice is literally the easiest thing you can do."

"I know."

"And Marcus isn't even a bad person. He's funny sometimes. He's smart. He could be so cool if he just... stopped. But instead he does stuff like this, and everyone laughs, and the kid who gets flicked just has to sit there and take it."

Priya looked at Nila with an expression Nila had seen before — the one that said *I hear you, and I love you, and also you might need to breathe.*

"Breathe," Priya said.

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing like you're running. Breathe like you're sitting."

Nila took a breath. A real one. The kind that goes all the way down instead of stopping at the throat.

The fire dimmed. Not out. Just... lower. Like turning a gas stove from high to medium.

"I hate that I care this much," Nila said quietly.

"I don't hate it," Priya said. "I just wish it didn't hurt you."

---

After school, Nila sat in the kitchen while Paati made murukku.

Watching Paati make murukku was one of the calmest things in the universe. The dough went into the press, Paati squeezed, and spirals emerged — perfect, golden, repeating. The oil sizzled. Paati's hands moved with a precision that came from forty years of practice, each spiral identical, each one placed in the oil at exactly the right moment.

"Paati, why are some people mean?"

Paati didn't look up from the press. Spiral. Squeeze. Drop. Sizzle.

"Mean to you?"

"Mean to everybody. There's a boy at school who... does things. Not terrible things. Small things. Flicking ears. Hiding people's stuff. Making fun of how people talk. And everyone laughs, and nobody stops him, and the people he does it to just have to... sit there."

Paati lifted a finished murukku from the oil — golden, perfect — and placed it on the rack.

"When Adi is hungry," Paati said, "he cries. Very loud. Until food comes."

"What does that have to do with —"

"Baby cries for food. Gets food. Learns: crying works." She started another spiral. "Some children cry for food. Some children cry for —" She tapped her eyes. "Looking. Seeing."

"Attention?"

"Attention. If cry gets attention, they cry more. If funny-mean gets attention..." She shrugged, the universal grandmother gesture for "you see where this goes."

"So Marcus is doing it because we react?"

"I don't know this Marcus. But I know: if something works, people do it again."

Nila took a murukku from the cooling rack and bit into it. Salty, crispy, warm. The fire in her chest had been slowly dimming since she sat down — the rhythm of the kitchen, the smell of the oil, Paati's steady presence — and now it was barely there. A 2, maybe. A memory of heat.

But the idea Paati had planted was growing.

*If something works, people do it again.*

Marcus flicked Jaden's ear. Nila reacted — loudly, visibly, the whole class watching. Marcus grinned.

What if the grin wasn't about Jaden? What if the grin was about her?

What if Nila's fire — her big, bright, impossible-to-miss fire — was the thing that made it work?

The murukku went stale in her mouth.

---

That night, Nila lay in bed and felt the antenna pick up the house.

Appa was watching cricket downstairs, the crowd noise rising and falling. He was happy — she could feel it, a warm hum coming through the floor. Amma was in the next room, talking softly on the phone to her sister in Chennai. Her voice had the particular quality of someone talking to someone they don't have to perform for — loose, laughing, younger. Adi was asleep already, his small-kid breathing audible through the thin wall.

And Paati was in her room, silent, probably awake. Paati slept when Paati wanted to and not a minute before.

Nila's antenna picked up all of it — the warmth, the laughter, the sleep, the quiet — and for once, the signals were gentle. The house was at peace. The fire was out. Her chest was soft.

But the question was still there, sitting in the space where the fire usually burned:

*If my reaction is what makes it work — if my caring, my fire, my loud impossible-to-ignore feeling — is the fuel...*

*Then what am I supposed to do? Stop caring?*

She didn't have an answer. The ceiling didn't have one either. But the question sat with her, warm and uncomfortable, like a stone heated by the sun.

She fell asleep holding it.

## Still Thinking?

- Nila says her "volume is stuck at 10." Do you feel things loudly or quietly? Is there a volume knob, or does everything come in at full blast?

- Paati calls Nila a "big antenna." If your antenna were a size, how big would it be? What signals does it pick up the strongest — happy, sad, angry, nervous?

- When Marcus flicked Jaden's ear, Nila's body reacted before she decided to react. Has that happened to you — where your body was already doing something before your brain caught up?

- Paati says "if something works, people do it again." Think about a time someone did something annoying or mean. Did they get a reaction? Do you think the reaction was what they were looking for?

- Nila asks: "Am I supposed to stop caring?" What do you think? Is there a difference between caring and reacting?
