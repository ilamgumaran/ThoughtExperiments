# Chapter 5: The Weight of Caring

---

On a rainy Tuesday in October, a girl named Sophie moved away.

Sophie wasn't Nila's close friend. They'd been in the same class since second grade and had that particular kind of school relationship where you knew each other's handwriting and favorite color but had never been to each other's houses. They were hallway friends. Lunch-wave friends. The kind of friends who existed in the background of each other's lives, steady and unremarkable.

Sophie's family was moving to Oregon. Her dad got a job. The move was fast — announced on Monday, gone by Friday. On Wednesday, Mrs. Callahan had the class sign a card.

Nila signed the card and cried.

Not a little. Not a polite tear. Her eyes filled and her throat closed and she had to go to the bathroom and press paper towels against her face while the big, heaving sobs worked their way through her body.

She wasn't even sad about Sophie specifically. She was sad about the IDEA of Sophie — a person you see every day who suddenly isn't there. The desk that would be empty. The handwriting that wouldn't appear on the group assignments. The particular way Sophie tucked her hair behind her ear when she was concentrating, which Nila had seen a thousand times and never once thought about until she realized she'd never see it again.

She was crying about impermanence. She was ten and she didn't know that word, but that's what it was — the enormous, unbearable fact that things end.

Priya found her in the bathroom.

"It's Sophie, isn't it."

"It's EVERYTHING. It's Sophie and it's the empty desk and it's the idea that anyone can just LEAVE and the world keeps going like they were never here."

"You weren't even that close."

"I KNOW. That's what makes it worse. If we were close, at least the crying would make sense. But I'm crying about someone I barely knew, and that means I'd cry about ANYONE, and that means everything is sad because everything ends, and I can't carry all of that."

Priya sat on the bathroom floor — which was disgusting, but she did it anyway, because that's what Priya did — and let Nila cry.

---

The Sophie incident opened something.

Not a door. A faucet. Once Nila started noticing impermanence, she couldn't stop. The leaves were falling off the trees outside the classroom, and each leaf was a tiny ending. Adi lost a tooth, and Nila cried about the baby tooth, the small white piece of Adi's childhood sitting in his palm. Paati mentioned that her sister in India was getting older, and Nila's chest caved in at the thought of Paati's sister, a woman she'd never met, getting old in a place she'd never been.

Her antenna was picking up everything, and "everything" included time, and time was merciless.

She was carrying the weight of every sad thing that entered her field. Not just the present sad things — the sad things that might happen, the sad things that had happened to strangers, the sad things that were structural and permanent and woven into the fabric of being alive.

It was too heavy.

She knew it was too heavy because her body started telling her. Her shoulders ached. Not injury-ache — weight-ache, the kind that comes from holding something you can't put down. Her sleep got worse. Her dreams were vivid and emotionally charged — she dreamt that the school building was slowly sinking into the ground, and everyone kept going to class as if it wasn't happening, and she was the only one who could see it.

"You look tired," Amma said one morning.

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine. You've been quiet all week."

"Quiet isn't the same as not fine."

But it was. And Nila knew it. She was drowning in other people's feelings, and the more she absorbed, the less of her was available for her own life.

---

"Paati, can empathy kill you?"

A dramatic question for a Wednesday afternoon. Paati was folding laundry — a task she approached with the geometric precision of an architect.

"Kill?"

"Not actually kill. But... can caring too much about other people hurt you? Like, physically?"

Paati folded a towel. Perfect rectangle. Set it on the pile.

"When I was young, in the village, there was a woman. Everyone came to her with problems. Husband problems. Money problems. Child problems. She listened to everyone. Felt everything."

"What happened?"

"She got sick. Not one sickness — many. Stomach. Head. Sleeping. The village doctor said nothing wrong. But she was..." Paati mimed deflation, hands pressing down. "Empty. She gave everything out. Nothing left inside."

"That's what I feel like."

Paati stopped folding. She looked at Nila with the full weight of her attention, which was considerable.

"You feel like empty?"

"Not empty. Heavy. Like I'm carrying bags that aren't mine. Sophie left and I cried more than Sophie's actual friends. Adi lost a tooth and I cried about time. I see a kid sitting alone at lunch and I can't eat because I'm so sad for them."

"You eat their sadness."

"Yes. Exactly. I eat it. And it fills me up and there's no room for my own stuff."

Paati sat down next to Nila. She took Nila's hands — small, warm, precise — and held them.

"Antenna," she said, tapping Nila's head. "Big antenna. But antenna has no..." She struggled. "No wall. No edge. Everything comes in. Everything."

"I can't build a wall. That would mean not caring."

"Not wall. Not wall." Paati thought. Then she stood up and went to the kitchen. She came back with a colander — the metal one with the small holes, used for draining rice.

She held it up. "This. Not wall. Not open. This."

"A strainer?"

"Water comes through. Rice stays. Feeling comes through — you feel. But the feeling goes through. You don't hold the water. You feel it and it goes."

"That's what you said before. The coffee filter."

"Same thing. You feel Sophie leaving. Sad comes in. You feel it. Sad passes through. What stays is" — she touched Nila's chest — "caring. Not the weight. The caring."

"But HOW? How do you let sadness pass through? I don't have a drain hole."

Paati laughed. A rare, full laugh. "You do. Everyone does. The hole is..." She pointed to Nila's feet. "Down. Into the ground. Like tree."

She put the colander down and held her hands up like branches. "Tree feels rain. Rain comes in — leaves, branches, trunk. Rain goes down — into roots, into ground. Tree doesn't hold rain. Tree lets rain go through. Tree stays standing."

"But I'm not a tree."

"No. You're better. Tree doesn't choose. You choose. You choose to feel. You choose to let through."

---

Nila tried it at school the next day.

At lunch, she saw a first-grader sitting alone. The kid was eating a sandwich with enormous concentration, like the sandwich was the most important thing in the world, and something about the smallness of him — the tiny shoes, the backpack that was almost bigger than he was — made Nila's antenna light up.

*He's alone. He's lonely. That's so sad.*

The sadness rushed in. Familiar. Heavy. Her shoulders started to pull down.

*Tree. Rain. Through.*

She felt the sadness — fully felt it, didn't push it away, didn't tell herself it was stupid. She let it land. And then she imagined it draining downward, through her chest, through her stomach, through her legs, into the ground.

The sadness didn't vanish. But it moved. It went from a weight sitting on her shoulders to a feeling passing through her body. Like the difference between holding a bucket of water and standing under a waterfall — the waterfall is more, but it keeps moving. The bucket stays.

What was left — the thing that stayed after the sadness drained — was caring. Pure caring. And caring, without the weight of sadness, was light. Mobile. It could do things.

Nila walked over to the first-grader.

"Hey. Want some company?"

The kid looked up. Small eyes. Big surprise. "Okay."

She sat with him. His name was Oliver. He was eating turkey and cheese. He liked dinosaurs and wasn't lonely at all — his friend was in the bathroom and he was saving a seat.

Nila laughed. Her antenna had read the signal wrong. The kid wasn't suffering. He was just small and eating a sandwich. But it didn't matter, because the interaction was good anyway — Oliver showed her his dinosaur eraser, and Nila told him she used to have a dinosaur shirt she loved, and for three minutes they were just two kids talking about dinosaurs.

The caring had done something useful. The sadness hadn't needed to.

---

That night, Nila wrote in her notebook:

*Things I absorbed today:*
- *Oliver's "loneliness" (not actually lonely)*
- *Mrs. Callahan's stress during the fire drill*
- *Adi's frustration with his math homework*
- *The general anxiety of the cafeteria*

*Things that passed through:*
- *All of them, eventually*

*Things that stayed:*
- *The caring. Just the caring.*

*I think the antenna isn't the problem. The antenna is fine. The problem is that I've been holding everything the antenna catches, like every signal is a rock I have to carry. But signals aren't rocks. They're rain. They come. They go through. What they water is already there.*

*I still cried about Sophie. But today, when I thought about her empty desk, I didn't feel heavy. I felt... warm. Like the caring was there without the weight. Like I could miss her without drowning in it.*

*That's new.*

## Still Thinking?

- Nila cries about Sophie even though they weren't close. She says she's "crying about impermanence — the idea that anyone can just leave." Have you ever been sadder about something than you expected? Was it about the thing itself, or about something bigger underneath?

- Nila says she "eats other people's sadness" and it fills her up. Have you ever absorbed someone else's feelings — picked up their stress or sadness without meaning to? What did it feel like in your body?

- Paati's colander: feelings come through, you feel them, and they pass. The caring stays. Does that match your experience, or do you find that some feelings are harder to let through than others? Which ones get stuck?

- Nila felt sad about the first-grader, let the sadness pass through, and what was left was caring — so she went and sat with him. The action came from caring, not from sadness. Think about a time you helped someone. What drove you — the feeling of their pain, or the desire to do something good?

- "Signals aren't rocks. They're rain." What's the difference between carrying every feeling you receive and letting them pass through? Which one do you do?
