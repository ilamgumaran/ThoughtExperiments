# Chapter 10: The Knowing

---

Something happened in December that tested everything Nila had learned.

A new kid transferred into Mrs. Callahan's class. His name was Ethan, and within one day, Nila knew — with the certainty of a person whose antenna never lied — that Ethan was different from Marcus.

Not different-good or different-bad. Different in the way a sharp knife is different from a plastic one. Marcus was a plastic knife: dull enough that he mostly caused messes, not wounds. Ethan was sharper.

---

Ethan was quiet. Polite to adults. He said "yes ma'am" to Mrs. Callahan and "thank you" to the lunch lady and held doors for teachers. Adults loved him within twenty-four hours.

But Nila's antenna picked up something in the gaps between his politeness. Something calculated. Not the desperate-attention thing Marcus had — Marcus was a puppy that bit too hard. Ethan was... deliberate.

On his second day, Ethan sat next to Aiden at lunch and said, casually, "Your friends talk about you behind your back. Just thought you should know."

Aiden's face collapsed. "Who? What did they say?"

"I don't want to get involved. Just looking out for you."

Nila heard this from two tables away. Her antenna locked on. The fire lit — but a different kind. Not the justice-fire. An alarm. The kind of alarm that isn't about anger but about danger.

Because here's what Ethan had done: he'd planted a seed of doubt in Aiden's head about his friends. And he'd done it while looking like a good guy. "Just looking out for you." Helpful. Concerned. Kind.

But Nila knew — she KNEW, in her chest, in her bones, in the part of her that felt everything — that Ethan wasn't looking out for Aiden. He was testing the room. Seeing which kid was vulnerable, which relationships had cracks, where the pressure points were.

She couldn't prove it. She couldn't point to a prank or a flicked ear or a pulled chair. Ethan hadn't done anything visible. He'd just spoken a sentence. A sentence that, to any teacher listening, sounded like one kid helping another.

---

"Something is wrong with the new kid," Nila told Priya.

"Ethan? He seems nice."

"He seems nice. That's the problem."

"Nila, you can't just decide someone is bad because they seem nice."

"I'm not deciding. I'm feeling. My antenna is going off and it's not the regular signal. It's the danger one."

Priya looked unconvinced. This was the problem with having an antenna that nobody else had — your data was invisible to everyone else.

"What specifically did he do?"

"He told Aiden that his friends talk about him behind his back."

"Maybe they do."

"Maybe. But Ethan has been here TWO DAYS. How would he know? And why would he tell Aiden instead of just staying out of it? He doesn't even know Aiden."

Priya considered this. Priya never dismissed things outright — she examined them, turned them over, looked for the angles.

"Okay. That is a little weird."

"And the way he said it. 'I don't want to get involved.' He IS involved. He MADE himself involved by saying it. That sentence is an involvement."

"So what do you think he's doing?"

"I think he's stirring. Like, with a spoon. He's dropping words into situations and stirring, and then sitting back to watch what happens."

"That sounds like Marcus."

"It's not like Marcus. Marcus is loud. Marcus wants the big reaction, the laugh, the scene. Ethan wants... I don't know. He wants something else. He wants people to turn on each other. And he wants to look clean while it happens."

---

Over the next week, Nila watched Ethan the way she'd watched Marcus. Notebook out. Scientist behind the glass. But this observation was harder, because Ethan was subtle.

**Incident 1:** Ethan told Cora that Lily said her science project was "basic." Cora confronted Lily. Lily denied it. They argued. Ethan watched from three desks away, face neutral, uninvolved. Nila checked with Lily later — Lily had never said it.

**Incident 2:** Ethan volunteered to be partners with Jaden on a project. For two days, he was great — helpful, focused, doing his share. Then on the third day, he told Mrs. Callahan that Jaden hadn't done any work. Jaden — who had done most of the work — was speechless. Ethan's face was concerned, regretful. "I didn't want to say anything, but I didn't want to get a bad grade."

**Incident 3:** Ethan sat with Marcus at lunch. This sent a chill through Nila's body. Because Marcus — hurting, attention-seeking, wall-up Marcus — was the most manipulable kid in the class. And Ethan, after twenty minutes of lunch conversation, had Marcus laughing. Not performing-laughing. Real laughing. Marcus liked Ethan. Marcus, who didn't trust easily, trusted Ethan.

Nila's alarm was at a constant 6.

---

"Paati, how do you know the difference between someone who is being nice and someone who is pretending to be nice?"

Paati was darning socks. An ancient skill that nobody under sixty seemed to possess.

"Why you ask?"

"There's a new boy at school. Everyone thinks he's nice. Teachers love him. But I feel something... off. And I can't explain it because he hasn't DONE anything. He's just said things. Small things. That cause big problems between other people."

Paati threaded her needle. "In the village, there was a man who sold coconuts. Everyone liked him. He smiled, he gave fair price, he remembered names."

"And?"

"And one day, we found out he was mixing good coconuts with rotten ones, inside. Outside — perfect. Inside — bad. But you only know when you open."

"So some people are rotten inside?"

"No. People are not coconuts." Paati smiled. "But some people learn: if outside is good, nobody checks inside. They learn: nice words, nice face, nobody looks deeper."

"So how do you check?"

"You watch what happens AROUND them. The coconut man — his customers got stomach-sick. Not from the good ones. From the rotten ones mixed in. You don't see the rotten coconut. You see the sick customer."

Nila thought about Cora fighting with Lily. About Jaden wrongly accused. About Marcus being drawn in.

"I see the sick customers," she said. "Everyone around Ethan ends up in conflict, and Ethan is always standing at the edge looking clean."

"Then your antenna is working."

---

But knowing and proving were different things.

Nila tried telling Mrs. Callahan. She chose her words carefully, used her slow-fire voice, and said, "I think Ethan is saying things to cause problems between kids."

Mrs. Callahan listened. She was a good teacher. She cared. But she said: "Can you give me a specific example? Something I can follow up on?"

And Nila couldn't. Because every specific example was plausibly deniable. "I was just trying to help." "I thought Aiden should know." "I didn't want to get a bad grade." Each sentence was constructed to look helpful. The damage was always one step removed.

"I'll keep an eye on it," Mrs. Callahan said.

But Mrs. Callahan had thirty kids and a curriculum and indoor recess four days that week and a parent-teacher conference schedule that looked like an air traffic control screen. "Keeping an eye on it" meant it would slide to the bottom of the pile.

Nila felt the old fire. The *do something, someone must do something* fire. But this time, she recognized it. And she recognized that the fire, aimed at this problem, wouldn't work. She couldn't yell Ethan into being honest. She couldn't confront him — he'd deflect with his nice-face. She couldn't prove anything because nothing was provable.

This was a different kind of unfairness. Not the visible kind. The invisible kind. The kind that a big antenna could feel but couldn't point to.

---

"I can't fix this one," Nila said to Priya.

They were in the library, theoretically studying, actually processing.

"Not yet," Priya said.

"Not 'not yet.' Maybe not ever. Some things can't be fixed by one kid in fifth grade."

"So what do you do?"

Nila thought about everything she'd learned. The fire. The stage. The coffee filter. The bridge. The caring without the burning. The shower instead of the jet.

"I protect the people I can protect," she said. "I told Aiden that nobody's talking about him — that Ethan made it up. I told Lily that Cora was upset because of something Ethan said, not something Lily did. I told Jaden I saw him do his share of the project."

"Did it help?"

"Aiden believed me. Lily and Cora talked and worked it out. Jaden..." Nila paused. "Jaden already knew. He said he noticed the pattern on day two."

"Of course he did."

"So I can't stop Ethan from being Ethan. But I can make sure the people around him have the real information."

Priya nodded. "That's not nothing, Nila."

"It's not everything."

"No. But since when is everything the requirement?"

---

That night, Nila wrote in her notebook:

*The difference between Marcus and Ethan:*

*Marcus is loud. His pain is visible. His pranks are obvious. You can see the stage, the spotlight, the show. You can choose not to walk on.*

*Ethan is quiet. His manipulation is invisible. There's no stage to walk off of because the stage doesn't look like a stage. It looks like a conversation. A helpful comment. A concerned face.*

*For Marcus, the answer was: don't give him the show.*
*For Ethan, the answer is: give everyone else the truth.*

*I can't fight every battle. I can't fix every person. I can't make the world safe by being loud enough or caring enough or angry enough.*

*But I can see clearly. My antenna — the big, loud, everything-absorbing antenna — isn't just for feeling. It's for knowing. For seeing what's real when everything looks fine on the surface.*

*The fire told me: fight him.*
*The knowing tells me: see him. See what he does. Protect who you can. Name what's happening to the people it's happening to.*

*The fire is one tool. The knowing is another. And sometimes the knowing is the braver one, because the knowing doesn't get to yell. The knowing just has to be right, and be patient, and trust that the truth is enough.*

## Still Thinking?

- Marcus is obvious and Ethan is subtle. Nila says the subtle kind of meanness is harder because "there's no stage to walk off of." Have you ever dealt with someone who was hurtful in ways that were hard to see or prove?

- Nila's antenna picks up something "off" about Ethan before she has evidence. Have you ever felt that something was wrong about a person or situation before you could explain why? Did you trust that feeling?

- Paati's coconut story: "You don't see the rotten coconut. You see the sick customer." What does it mean to look at the effects instead of the person? Is that a reliable way to know the truth?

- Nila can't fix Ethan, so she protects the people around him by sharing the truth. Is that enough? What would you do in her situation?

- "The fire is one tool. The knowing is another." Which one do you use more? Which one do you trust more?
