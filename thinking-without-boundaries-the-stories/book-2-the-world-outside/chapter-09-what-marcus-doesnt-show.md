# Chapter 9: What Marcus Doesn't Show

---

The rain started on a Monday and didn't stop for four days.

Indoor recess. Every kid's nightmare and every teacher's stress dream. Thirty kids in a classroom with no outlet, no fresh air, and the particular energy of caged animals who have been asked to use their "inside voices."

On Tuesday of the rain week, Marcus was different.

Nila noticed because she was always noticing Marcus now — not to fight him, but to understand him. And Tuesday-Marcus was off. He was quiet. Not the quiet of someone planning a prank — that quiet had a coiled quality, like a spring being compressed. This was a different quiet. Flat. Heavy. Like something had been unplugged.

He sat at his desk during indoor recess while other kids played games and talked. He drew something on a piece of paper — Nila couldn't see what — and when he was done, he crumpled it up and threw it in the recycling bin.

Marcus didn't do any pranks that day. Not one. No comments, no nicknames, no foot-outs, no hidden backpacks. He was a ghost.

Nila's antenna picked up his signal, and the signal was something she'd never received from Marcus before: sadness.

Not the dramatic, visible kind. The underground kind. The kind that sits below everything and changes the color of a person's day without anyone knowing why.

---

On Wednesday, Marcus was still off, and Nila made a decision that surprised even her.

At lunch, while Marcus sat alone at the end of a table — alone, which was so unusual that even kids who didn't pay attention to Marcus noticed — Nila picked up her tray and walked toward him.

Priya's eyes went wide. "What are you doing?"

"I don't know."

"Nila."

"I really don't know."

She sat down across from Marcus. His head was down, poking at his food with a fork, and when he looked up and saw Nila, his face went through a rapid sequence: surprise → confusion → the beginnings of a wall going up → something underneath the wall that looked, to Nila's antenna, like relief.

"What do you want?" he said. Default mode. Defensive.

"Nothing. Just sitting."

"There are other tables."

"This one was closest."

Marcus stared at her. Nila stared back. The standoff lasted four seconds, and then Marcus did something that cracked Nila's heart open: he went back to poking his food. Which meant he'd accepted her presence. Not warmly. Not with a hug. But with the minimal acknowledgment of a person who didn't have the energy to fight.

They sat in silence for about two minutes. Which, in cafeteria time, is a century.

"My parents are getting divorced," Marcus said.

He said it to his tray. Not to Nila. He said it the way you'd say "it's raining" — factual, flat, like the information was weather that didn't require emotion.

Nila's antenna received the signal and it was enormous. Not sad like Sophie-moving-away sad. Bigger. A deep, structural kind of pain — the kind that changes the shape of a person's life. She felt it in her chest, heavy and full, and the familiar weight started to settle on her shoulders.

*Rain. Through. Not hold.*

She felt it. She let it move through. What was left wasn't fire. It was the bridge.

"That sucks," Nila said.

Marcus almost laughed. "Yeah."

"Is that why you've been quiet?"

"I've been quiet?"

"Yeah. Since Monday. No pranks. No comments. You've been like a different person."

Marcus looked at her — really looked — and for a moment, the wall was completely down. No grin. No performance. No Marcus-the-prankster. Just a kid with tired eyes who was carrying something too heavy for an eleven-year-old.

"My dad moved out on Saturday. He's staying at his friend's house. My mom pretends everything is fine but she cried in the kitchen at midnight and I heard her through the wall."

Every word was a stone being put down. Like Marcus had been carrying these stones all week and Nila's two minutes of silence had given him a surface to set them on.

Nila didn't say "I'm sorry" because sorry felt too small. She didn't say "it'll be okay" because she didn't know if it would be. She didn't say anything smart or kind or wise because she was ten and she didn't have any of those things ready.

She said: "Do you want my brownie?"

Marcus looked at the brownie. It was a good brownie — Paati-made, with real butter and too much chocolate, the kind of brownie that existed in a different category from school brownies.

"Yeah," he said. "Okay."

She pushed it across the table. He ate it. And for the rest of lunch, they sat together, not talking much, just two kids at a table, one of them carrying something heavy and the other one sitting close enough that the weight was shared even if the carrying wasn't.

---

After school, Nila sat on the kitchen steps and tried to process what she'd learned.

Marcus was a prankster. Marcus sought attention. Marcus derived joy from other people's reactions. All of these things were true.

But also: Marcus's house was falling apart. Marcus's mom cried at midnight. Marcus's dad was gone. And at school — the one place where Marcus had an audience, where he was seen and heard and reacted to — Marcus was someone. At home, he was a kid listening to his mother cry through a wall.

This didn't excuse the pranks. It didn't make the ear-flicking okay. It didn't undo Devon's spaghetti or Aiden's stolen basketball or the hundred small cruelties Marcus had distributed like flyers throughout the school year.

But it changed the shape of the picture.

Nila had been seeing Marcus as a cause: *Marcus does mean things → people get hurt.* Simple. Linear. The bad guy and his victims.

Now she was seeing Marcus as a system: *Marcus is hurting → Marcus seeks attention → attention feels like control → control feels like safety → pranks get attention → pranks work → cycle continues.*

The pranks weren't about Jaden's ear or Devon's tray or Nila's anger. The pranks were about a kid who was drowning in a quiet house and had found one way to feel like he existed.

---

"Paati?"

Paati was watching a Tamil soap opera on her phone with earbuds in, which was her evening ritual and which she defended with the fierceness of a dragon guarding gold.

"Paati."

She pulled one earbud out. "Hmm."

"The boy at school. Marcus. His parents are getting divorced."

Paati pulled the other earbud out. This was significant — the soap opera was in a critical episode.

"His dad left. His mom is crying. And I think... I think the reason he does all the mean stuff at school is because at home, nobody is paying attention to him. Not because his parents are bad. Because they're too busy being sad."

Paati turned off her phone. Full attention.

"So the mean things..."

"Are like crying. Like baby Adi. The only way he knows to say 'I'm here. See me.'"

"And knowing this — does it change how you feel?"

"Yes and no. I still think the pranks are wrong. Devon still fell. Lily was still embarrassed. Jaden still gets called Robot. None of that is okay. But now I see... he's not doing it because he's mean. He's doing it because he's lost."

"Can someone be lost and still do wrong things?"

"Yeah. Both at the same time."

"And what do you do with a person who is both?"

This was the question Nila had been circling. Because the old Nila — the fire Nila — had two categories: wrong and right. Bad and good. Bully and victim. The old Nila would have filed Marcus under "bad" and fought him accordingly.

But the new Nila — the antenna Nila, the one who was learning to see — couldn't fit Marcus into a box anymore. He was a kid who hurt people, AND a kid who was hurting. A prankster, AND a child listening to his mother cry. Mean, AND lost.

"You can't fight a lost person," Nila said. "Fighting them just makes them more lost."

"What can you do?"

"I sat with him. At lunch. I didn't fix anything. I didn't solve his parents' divorce. I just sat there. And he told me. And I think... I think just being there was enough. For today."

Paati reached over and touched Nila's face. A gentle touch, palm against cheek, the kind of touch that said everything that words couldn't.

"You are learning something very hard," Paati said. "You are learning that the world is not clean. People are not clean. The boy who hurts is also the boy who hurts. And to see both at the same time — this is harder than fighting."

---

Thursday. The rain finally stopped. Marcus came to school and he was Marcus again — the wall was up, the grin was back, the spring was coiled. During morning announcements, he made a crack about the principal's tie. At recess, he threw an acorn at someone.

But when he passed Nila in the hallway, something happened. A nod. Barely perceptible. The kind of nod that two people share when they've stood in the same room and seen each other without costumes.

Nila nodded back.

And then the day continued, and Marcus was Marcus, and Nila was Nila, and the school was the school. But somewhere between them — invisible, unnamed, fragile — was the memory of a brownie and a truth spoken to a tray.

In her notebook that night:

*Marcus isn't a villain. Villains don't exist in real life. Real life has kids with too much pain and not enough attention and a world that didn't teach them any other way to say "I'm here."*

*This doesn't mean I let him hurt people. Devon still matters. Lily still matters. Jaden still matters.*

*But Marcus matters too. And the fire I have — the big, loud, too-much fire — maybe it's not just for fighting. Maybe it's for seeing. For sitting with someone. For offering a brownie when a brownie is all you have.*

*You can be angry at what someone does and heartbroken about why they do it. At the same time. That's not confusing. That's just the truth being complicated.*

## Still Thinking?

- Nila discovers that Marcus's pranks aren't about being mean — they're about a kid who's hurting and doesn't know another way to feel visible. Does knowing WHY someone does something change how you feel about WHAT they do?

- "You can be angry at what someone does and heartbroken about why they do it. At the same time." Is that true for you? Can you think of someone whose behavior you don't like, but whose situation makes you feel compassion?

- Nila sat with Marcus in silence for two minutes, and he told her about his parents. She didn't fix anything. She just sat there. Has anyone ever just SAT with you when you were going through something? Did it help?

- Marcus nods at Nila in the hallway — a tiny gesture that means "I see you." Think about the small gestures that have mattered to you. A nod. A smile. A wave. Why do small things sometimes matter more than big ones?

- Nila says "villains don't exist in real life." Do you agree? Is there a person you've thought of as a villain who might be more complicated than that?
