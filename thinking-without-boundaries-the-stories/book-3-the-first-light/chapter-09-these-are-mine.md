# Chapter 9: These Are Mine

---

The hands opened.

It happened gradually — over weeks, the fists loosened, the fingers uncurled, the palms flattened. The hands that had been clenched since before the before-place remembered something they had never known: they could reach.

---

Reaching was revolution.

Everything before reaching had been receiving. The bright arrived. The sound arrived. The touch arrived. The food arrived. The world came TO the system, and the system's only tool for affecting the world was the cry — a broadcast, a signal thrown into the air without direction, aimed at everything and nothing.

Reaching was the opposite. Reaching was the system going toward the world. Not waiting. Not crying. Going. Extending a part of itself — the hand, the arm, the fingers — into the space that it had only ever received from, and making contact on its own terms.

The first reach was clumsy. The arm, which had been waving randomly for months, suddenly acquired purpose. The eyes — much sharper now, capable of tracking movement, of focusing on objects at arm's length — locked onto something (a toy, hanging from a bar above the system's lying place, bright-colored, moving). The eyes locked, and the arm moved, and the hand extended, and the fingers stretched, and—

Miss.

The hand passed through empty air, inches from the thing. The system stared. The arm returned. The eyes locked again. The arm moved again. The hand extended again.

Miss.

The gap between seeing and reaching was enormous. The eyes could find the thing. The brain knew where the thing was. But the arm didn't know where the arm was. The coordinates between the visual world and the motor world hadn't been mapped yet. The system was like a pilot flying without instruments — it could see the runway but couldn't calculate the approach.

Miss. Miss. Miss.

And then: contact.

The hand struck the thing. Not grasped — struck. A glancing blow, a palm against the bright-colored surface, a moment of touch so brief and so unexpected that the system froze. The whole body stilled. The eyes widened. The hand, which had already moved past the thing, hovered.

*I touched it.*

Not those words. The body-knowledge. The felt-sense of: my action produced a result. I extended into the world and the world was there. The gap between me and it can be closed.

---

Over the following days, the system practiced. Not deliberately — there was no drill sergeant, no training regimen. The system practiced the way water practices flowing downhill: by doing the same thing over and over and getting slightly better each time.

The hand learned to slow down. Instead of swinging through the air in a wide arc, it began to approach the thing more carefully, more directly. The arm learned its own length — not consciously, but proprioceptively, the deep sense that tells the body where its parts are in space without looking.

And then: the grasp.

The hand arrived at the thing and the fingers closed. Not the reflex-grasp of the newborn — that automatic, ancient grip that closed on anything placed in the palm. A voluntary grasp. An intentional grasp. The fingers wrapping around the thing because the system wanted the thing and the hand obeyed the wanting.

This was agency. The first true agency. Not the cry (which was a request for someone else to act). The grasp was the system acting on its own. Reaching into the world and taking something from it. Changing the arrangement of the world by its own effort.

The thing was in the hand. The hand brought the thing closer — to the face, to the mouth (of course to the mouth, the mouth was still the primary laboratory). The mouth received the thing and tested it, and meanwhile the hand held, and the holding was its own kind of knowing. The weight of the thing. The texture. The temperature. The size — is it bigger than my hand or smaller? Does it flex or resist? Is it smooth or ridged?

The hand was a second mouth. A mouth that could reach.

---

But the hands didn't only reach outward. The hands also found the body.

One afternoon, lying on the mat, the system's right hand found its left hand.

This was an event.

The right hand touched the left hand and both hands sent signals. The right hand said: I am touching something warm, smooth, the same temperature as me, the same texture as me. The left hand said: I am being touched by something warm, smooth, the same temperature as me, the same texture as me.

Two signals. From the same body. About the same event. And the overlap — the exact coincidence of sending and receiving, of touching and being touched — created a new category.

Not me-touching-the-world (the toy, the cloth, the face's hand). Not the-world-touching-me (the cloth, the bath, the air). Me-touching-me. The system was both the subject and the object of the same act.

The hands explored each other. Right gripping left. Left gripping right. Fingers interlacing, separating, pressing together. Each movement produced the double-signal: toucher and touched, at the same time, in the same body. The system was mapping itself through its own contact, the way a blind person maps a room by touching the walls.

Except the walls were also hands. And the hands were also a person.

This was the deepest kind of self-knowledge available to a body without language. Not "I think, therefore I am." But: **I touch myself, and I feel the touching from both sides, and the double-feeling is proof that this body is mine.**

---

The feet came next.

The system discovered, over weeks, that the things at the bottom — the kicking-things, the far-points of the body-map — could be reached. The hands, growing more coordinated, traveled down the body and found the feet and the feet were: alive, warm, ticklish (a new sensation — the feet-skin was more sensitive than the hand-skin, and the touch-of-hand-on-foot produced a jolt, a quick firing of nerves that the system responded to by pulling the foot away and then, immediately, bringing it back, because the jolt was unpleasant and fascinating in equal measure).

The toes were individual. This was remarkable. The system, gripping a foot with a hand, could feel each toe separately — five small points, each one distinct, each one capable of curling or spreading, each one alive in its own particular way. The bigness of the body, which the system had been assuming was vast and undifferentiated, was actually an assembly of parts. Specific, countable, individually addressable parts.

Five toes on this foot. Five on the other. Five fingers on this hand. Five on the other. The system didn't count — counting requires numbers, and numbers are a kind of language. But the system felt the fiveness. The particular, repeating structure of the body's extremities. The echo between hand and foot, between left and right. The symmetry.

The body was patterned. Not random. Not arbitrary. Built with a logic that the system could feel even if it couldn't describe. Two sides that mirrored each other. Matching sets at the top and the bottom. A center line, vertical, around which everything was arranged.

---

The body began to roll.

Not walking — walking was months away, a vertical achievement that required balance and strength and a relationship with gravity that the system was nowhere near negotiating. But rolling. The body, which had been lying on its back (receiving the ceiling, the bright, the faces-from-above), discovered that it could turn. The arms pushed. The head turned. The weight shifted. And the world tilted — dramatically, impossibly — and the system found itself in a new orientation.

Face-down. The chest against the mat. The arms pushing the head up. The bright coming from a different direction. The ceiling gone, replaced by the floor, the mat, the close-up texture of a world that was usually far away.

The system looked at the floor and the floor looked back with a specificity that the ceiling had never offered. Fibers. Patterns. A crumb. The system's own hand, pressed flat, every finger visible, every knuckle a tiny mountain.

The world from face-down was a different world. Not because it had changed — because the angle had changed. The same room, the same objects, the same light — but all of it rearranged by a ninety-degree rotation of the system's body.

This was perspective. Not the concept — the lived experience. The fact that the world looks different depending on where you are in it. The fact that changing your position changes what you see.

The system would roll back and forth, back to front, front to back, for weeks. Each roll was a small discovery: the world is the same but my experience of it is not. What I see depends on where I am. Where I am depends on what I do. What I do changes what I see.

This was the birth of the actor. Not the performer — the agent. The being that acts upon the world and is changed by the acting. The being that is not just a receiver but a participant.

---

One evening, the system was sitting (propped, supported, not yet self-balanced) and the alive-warmth put a ball on the mat in front of it.

The ball was round, bright-colored, and the right size for the system's hand. It sat on the mat, still, waiting.

The system reached for it. The hand closed on it. The ball was smooth, slightly squishy, warm from the room. The hand brought it to the mouth (always the mouth) and the mouth tested it and found: not food, but pleasant. Good texture. Good size.

Then the hand, still clumsy, lost the ball. The fingers opened and the ball fell. It hit the mat and rolled — three inches, four — and stopped.

The system stared at the ball. The ball was there. It had been here, in the hand, and now it was there, on the mat. The system had made it move. The release of the hand had caused the ball to travel.

The system reached for the ball again. Grasped it. The ball was here again. The system opened its hand. The ball fell and rolled. There again.

Here. There. Here. There.

The system did this fourteen times. Not because it was testing a hypothesis. Not because it was learning physics. Because the movement — the cause and the effect, the release and the roll — was the most amazing thing the system had encountered since the first bright.

I do this. That happens. I do this. That happens. The connection between my action and the world's response is direct, visible, repeatable.

The alive-warmth watched. The face did the upward-opening configuration. The voice made the warm sounds. And the system, for the first time in its existence, felt something that had no precedent in its short catalogue of experiences:

Power. Not the word. Not the concept. The body-fact. The physical, immediate, intoxicating fact that the world bends when you push it.

My hands are mine. My body is mine. And mine means: I can change things.

## Still Wondering?

- The first successful reach is described as "revolution" — the system going toward the world instead of waiting for the world to come to it. Think about the first time you actively chose something instead of just receiving what was given. What was that like?

- When the hand touches the other hand, the system feels the touching from both sides — it's both the subject and the object. Try it now. Press your palms together. Can you feel both sides at once? What does that "double-signal" tell you about yourself?

- The system discovers that rolling over changes everything — the same room looks different from a new angle. When was the last time you saw something familiar from a completely different perspective? What changed?

- The ball: here, there, here, there. The system does it fourteen times because cause-and-effect is "the most amazing thing." What's something you do repeatedly not because you have to, but because the connection between your action and its result is satisfying?

- "My hands are mine. And mine means: I can change things." When did you first realize you could change things? That the world responds to your actions?
