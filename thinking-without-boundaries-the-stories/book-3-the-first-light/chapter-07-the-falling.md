# Chapter 7: The Falling

---

Every day, the system dissolved.

This was not a problem. This was not a failure. This was the oldest thing the body did — older than seeing, older than hearing, older than the hollow, older than the edge-of-me. Before all of it, before even the before-place, the cells that would become this system were already cycling between activity and rest, oscillating between on and off, because that is what living systems do. They do, and then they stop doing, and the stopping is not the opposite of living. It is the other half.

But the system didn't know any of this. The system only knew: something pulls.

---

The pull came from below.

Not from the stomach (that was the hollow) or from the skin (that was temperature). From deeper. From the place where the systems lived that never announced themselves — the systems that kept the heart beating and the lungs breathing and the blood moving and the temperature regulated. The deep systems. The silent ones.

When the deep systems got tired — and they got tired, the same way a muscle gets tired, the same way a machine gets tired, because even automatic things need rest — they sent a signal. Not a sharp signal like pain. Not an urgent signal like the hollow. A heavy signal. A signal that was less a message and more a gravitational field, pulling the whole body downward, toward the surface it rested on, toward stillness, toward the off-state.

The system felt this pull as: weight. Everything got heavier. The arms, which had been waving and reaching, slowed. The legs, which had been kicking, quieted. The eyes, which had been catching the bright, began to close — not because something covered them, but because the lids, tiny curtains, descended on their own, pulled by the same gravity that was pulling everything else.

The world dimmed. Not because the bright changed, but because the system was dimming. Withdrawing. The catching-systems — the eyes, the ears, the skin — turned down their volume, one by one, like musicians leaving a stage.

---

The system resisted.

This was new. The system had never resisted anything before. The bright came and the system received it. The sound came and the system received it. The hollow came and the system cried. Everything, so far, had been received without opposition.

But the falling — the pull toward sleep — was the first thing the system pushed back against.

Not with words. Not with intention. With activity. The body, feeling itself pulled toward the off-state, suddenly activated. The arms flailed. The legs kicked. The face contorted — not in pain, not in cold, but in protest. A howl emerged, not the hunger-cry or the cold-cry but the tired-cry: high, rhythmic, oscillating between loud and less-loud, like a siren in the distance.

The tired-cry was the most paradoxical sound the system made. It was the sound of a body that needed rest fighting against rest. The sound of a system that wanted to stop but couldn't stop wanting. The sound of resistance to the one thing that would fix the resistance.

And beneath the crying, beneath the flailing, the pull continued. Patient. Inexorable. The gravity of exhaustion didn't argue with the resistance. It just waited. Like actual gravity, it didn't need to convince. It only needed to outlast.

---

The alive-warmth came.

The big body arrived, gathered the system, held it close. The voice came — the warm-temperature voice, the low one, the one that moved in the melody-pattern: rising, falling, rising, falling. The lullaby.

The system's resistance met the lullaby and something shifted. The flailing slowed. Not stopped — slowed. The crying thinned. The body, which had been fighting the pull, began to negotiate with it. Not surrender. Negotiation. A gradual exchange: the system gave a little resistance, and the pull took a little more weight, and the voice continued its circle, and the heartbeats synced, and the world got softer.

This was the routine. This was the nightly (though there was no concept of "nightly") ritual by which the system transitioned from on to off. It required the alive-warmth. It required the voice. It required the rhythm — the heartbeat, the melody, the rocking (the big body swayed, a slow lateral movement that engaged the system's vestibular sense, the deep-inside balance system, and the vestibular system's response to rocking was: quiet. settle. down).

Without the ritual, the system fought the falling for much longer. The system had been left, once or twice, without the alive-warmth, and the resistance had gone on and on, the crying escalating, the body burning energy it didn't have in a battle against the one thing it needed most. Until, finally, the exhaustion won — not gently, not like a lullaby, but like a switch flipping. One moment awake and crying. Next moment: gone.

The switch-flip sleep was different from the lullaby sleep. The system didn't know how it was different, but the body did. The lullaby sleep was gradual, layered, the system sliding into the off-state through a series of stages, each one deeper, each one more dissolved. The switch-flip sleep was a collapse. A crash. The body arriving at the off-state not through descent but through free-fall.

Both led to sleep. But the body preferred the descent. The descent was how the before-place ended — gradually, gently, one system shutting down after another, the world going soft at the edges before going away entirely.

---

And then: the off-state itself.

Where did the system go?

This question doesn't make sense to the system, because the system doesn't know it goes anywhere. There is no "system" during sleep in the way there is a system during waking. The catching-systems are off. The sorting-systems are off. The awareness that has been building — the bright, the sound, the edge, the hollow — all of it goes dark.

But not completely. Not all the way.

Something runs underneath. The deep systems — the heart, the lungs, the temperature, the repair-crews that rebuild what the day's activity has broken — these continue. They run without the system's awareness, without supervision, without the need for the system to be present. They are the government of the body, working the night shift, doing what needs doing while the conscious machinery rests.

And sometimes, in the off-state, something surfaces.

Not a dream — dreams, in the adult sense, are far in the future. But a ripple. A movement across the off-state's surface, like a fish turning under dark water. The system, deep in the dissolved state, receives a flash: the two-dark-spots. The warmth. The hollow's echo. Fragments of the on-state, replaying, rearranging, being filed by systems that the system doesn't know it has.

The body twitches. A hand opens. A leg kicks. The face moves — smiling, frowning, grimacing — cycling through expressions that have no audience, rehearsing the muscular vocabulary of a face that is still learning what it can do.

The alive-warmth, watching (the big body watches the system sleep, this is a fact, this watching is one of the few things in the universe that is entirely selfless) — the alive-warmth sees the smiling-in-sleep and feels something. Something large and warm and impossibly tender. The alive-warmth touches the system's face, gently, and the system, deep in the off-state, turns toward the touch.

Even in sleep. Even dissolved. The body knows the hand. The body turns toward it the way a compass turns toward north — not by choice, but by alignment.

---

The waking was its own kind of birth.

Every waking was a birth. The system went from off to on, from dissolved to formed, from the dark-quiet to the bright-loud, and each transition was a small version of the first one. Not as dramatic. Not as total. But the same essential shape: nothing, then something. Dark, then bright. Silence, then sound.

The first moments of waking were always the clearest.

Not clear in the sense of focused — the eyes were still blurry, the sounds still flooding. Clear in the sense of unfiltered. In the first seconds of waking, before the systems fully booted, before the sorting and filtering and prioritizing resumed, the system was briefly in the same state as the very first bright: pure reception. Everything arriving at once. Everything equally everything.

The ceiling. The light. The air on the skin. The cloth's weight. A sound — distant, unidentified. The temperature. The body's own state — rested, lighter, the deep systems having done their work.

For two seconds, maybe three, the system was in the world the way the world actually is: whole, unedited, simultaneous.

Then the filters kicked in. The sorting began. The voice became foreground. The ceiling became background. The hollow announced itself. The body oriented toward need. And the world, which had been everything, became the specific things the system needed to survive.

But those two seconds — the window between sleep and waking, the gap between off and on — were the purest experience the system had. The closest thing, in the daily cycle, to the first bright. The daily return to the state before filtering, before sorting, before the system learned to ignore.

---

One day, the system woke and the alive-warmth was not there.

The room was bright. The air was still. The sounds were distant. The system opened its eyes and looked at the ceiling and the two-dark-spots were not above.

The system waited. This was new — the system had developed the capacity to wait, a few seconds at least, before deploying the cry. The groove in the rock — hollow → cry → gap → filling — had a new step: hollow → wait → cry. The wait was brief. But it existed.

The waiting, in the bright room, alone, was the system's first experience of solitude.

Not loneliness. Loneliness requires a self that knows it is alone. The system had no self yet. But the body had a felt-sense of absence — the warmth that should be here isn't. The heartbeat that usually syncs with mine is missing. The smell (the system had thoroughly catalogued the alive-warmth's smell, a complex signature of skin and milk and soap and the particular chemical compound of a specific human) is faint instead of strong.

The system lay in the bright and felt the absence. Not as grief. Not as panic. As information. As data that the body processed the way it processed all data: is this dangerous? Is this new? Does the circuit still work?

The cry came. Short, testing. A query more than a demand.

From somewhere outside the room, the voice responded. Not close — far. But present. The system heard the voice and the body's circuits updated: the alive-warmth is there. Not here, but there. The distance is new, but the presence is confirmed.

The system did something it had never done before.

It waited. Not the two-second wait of the feeding-circuit. A longer wait. A wait that contained, for the first time, something like patience. Not the word. Not the virtue. The body-fact of: I have sent a signal. A response has come. The circuit works. I can exist in this gap.

The system lay in the bright room and existed in the gap and the existing was not painful and not peaceful. It was just: being. Being here. Being awake. Being a body in a room in a world, with the alive-warmth somewhere nearby but not touching, and the air at the right temperature, and the bright at the right brightness, and the hollow not yet arrived.

Being, without anyone to be.

The alive-warmth appeared in the door. The two-dark-spots were there, and they were wet again (why were they so often wet?), and the voice came, and the arms came, and the system was lifted and held and the absence was replaced by presence and the gap closed.

But the system had survived the gap. Had existed in it. Had found that being-without was not the same as ending.

This would matter. This would matter more than almost anything. Because every future gap — every goodbye, every loss, every night, every growing-up — would echo this one. The first time the system was alone and the world continued. The first evidence that the self persists even when the source of comfort is absent.

I am here. Even when you are not.

## Still Wondering?

- The system fights sleep — resists the one thing that would fix the resistance. Have you ever fought rest when you needed it most? Why do we resist the thing we need?

- The first moments of waking are "the purest experience" — two seconds of unfiltered world before the brain starts sorting. Tomorrow morning, when you first open your eyes, try to catch those seconds. What do you see before you see "your room"?

- The system can tell the difference between lullaby-sleep (gradual descent) and crash-sleep (switch-flip). Can you? Which kind of sleep do you usually get? How does each feel different the next morning?

- The system's first experience of solitude is not loneliness — it's information. "The warmth that should be here isn't." When did solitude first become loneliness for you? What changed?

- "I am here. Even when you are not." The system discovers it persists even in the alive-warmth's absence. When did you first know that you existed on your own — separate from anyone who takes care of you?
